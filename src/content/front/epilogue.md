---
title: "Epilogue. Pre-capitalists, anti-capitalists"
order: 2
---

The reader will allow me, very briefly, to shed some light on what I understand to be behind many of the stories in this work. I’ll do this by means of two categories that I must acknowledge from the outset are sufficiently nebulous to encompass whatever we might subjectively deem appropriate, and exclude what, just as subjectively, doesn’t interest us.

The first of these categories, which will receive the bulk of my attention here, is associated with the world, societies, and cultures of the *pre-capitalist* era. It’s hardly an unproblematic category. For one, it seems to place capitalism at the center of history, envisaging societies as either pre-capitalist or, perhaps, post-capitalist. It also seems to assume, against all evidence, that a variety of extremely different realities can be encompassed under a single term. Finally, we must not be naive about the past, and remember that while there are aspects of pre-capitalist societies that are positive, these have often been accompanied by other qualities that are less so.

As for the second category, that of *anti-capitalism*, it also comes with its own set of complications, largely tied to the question of the degree to which the object of investigation is truly anti-capitalist. For example, while the social democracy of a century ago had a vision that could legitimately be called anti-capitalist, it seems impossible to say the same of the contemporary social-democracy. Much of the institutional left – I'm aware that the term is problematic – appears to be more anti-neoliberal than genuinely anti-capitalist. What, then, to make of the Soviet-type systems, often described as forms of state bureaucratic capitalism?

Despite these and perhaps other shortcomings, I believe it's useful to retain these two categories for purposes of classification. I often venture to suggest – as I do in one of the texts included in this work – that a significant part of the enduring appeal of the anarchist movement in Spain before 1939 is its manifestly spontaneous blend of pre-capitalist and anti-capitalist elements. The former is likely a consequence of the fact that its members came from rural areas, or were descendants of people from such backgrounds, who preserved in their mindset and behavior many aspects of collective organization and mutual aid.  This was coupled to the powerful influence of an ideology, anarchism, that emerged in 19th-century Europe and significantly strengthened pre-capitalist elements through a reciprocal influence.  A similar hybridization phenomenon is visible in recent initiatives like those in Chiapas and Rojava, among many examples.

Whatever the case, I want to emphasize that the pre-capitalist impulse has a more experiential and material dimension than the anti-capitalist one, where the weight of ideology is undoubtedly heavier. As Raúl Iturra says wisely, "the oral tradition of the peasant is not found in what he says, but in what he does.”  I also believe that by establishing a future combination of these two impulses, we will gain a compelling guide for navigating the stormy seas of revolutions and emancipations.  My point here is that in many of the stories collected in this book it’s relatively easy to see the imprint of a pre-capitalist impulse – its strength is greater in the peasant world and in Southern countries – whereas at other times, we detect the influence of an anti-capitalist viewpoint.

I’m compelled to delve into the nature of the pre-capitalist universe, which ultimately is what interests me here. In his writings, E. P. Thompson often used the concept of *moral economy* to refer to a set of realities that, in conditions characterized by the absence of nationalisms and of homogenizing religious orthodoxies, gave rise to a vast diversity of practices. These emerged under norms that were not necessarily in line with those dictated by the political or religious authorities of the time. In essence, the moral economy aimed, and aims, to protect the community against risk and often became manifest as resistance to technical innovations – recall the significant role of Luddism – and against the rationalizations of the market. Paul Greenough has defined moral economy as "the cluster of relations of exchange between social groups, and between persons, in which the welfare and the merit of both parties to the exchange take precedence over considerations such as the profit of the one or the other." Many of Thompson's works identify in 18th-, and particularly in 19th-century England, a confrontation between the emerging market economy on one side,  and the moral economy of the common people on the other, which resisted the intrusions of the bourgeoisie and the clergy.

The resistances in question were primarily materialized as collective work, thus blurring the separation between work and life, which in practice were intermingled. Moreover, in a context where self-subsistence was the general norm, the purpose of working wasn’t to benefit the market. This clearly highlighted the logic of solidarity, and of various forms democratic and assembly-oriented organizations, similar to the councils that still exist among us. Additionally, networks extended beyond the family, supporting a collective, cooperative effort.  Importantly, in social organization, and in subsequent uprisings, women had an active presence that later vanished into the market economy, with its fictitious universalist and liberating claims. The forms of resistance I discuss often appeared as irony, mockery, and subtle acts of defiance against the powerful, as might be illustrated in some of the stories in this book.

It's true that not everything glittered in the glow of this moral economy. In Western Europe, at least, the moral economy developed in an environment to varying degrees influenced by the interests of nobles, lords, landowners, and clergy, who sought to maintain many characteristics of societies in which inequality was clearly pervasive. Although there was an intent to provide some degree of security and to ensure a minimum income and land access, which did foster egalitarian principles to some extent, and though the wealthy were willing to sacrifice a portion of their profits to avoid unrest and rebellion, it is clear that repressive mechanisms serving the powerful persisted.

In tune with the interpretation I've suggested regarding pre-1939 Spanish anarchism, Michael Löwy has made an effort to highlight significant links between the world of pre-capitalist moral economy and the culture of the working class that emerged in the 19th century. Löwy cites Richard Hoggart in this regard, who has pointed out that many of the roots of working-class culture are indeed peasant in nature. Working-class culture has largely drawn from the values of local and family community, and affective ties, as opposed to modernity, which always refers to a society burdened by commerce, competition, selfishness, and corruption. This doesn't negate Peter Laslett's assertion that the advent of the modern world ended another world "in which entire life unfolded within the family, amid a circle of familiar and beloved faces, of objects known and cherished, all within man's reach." I'll add that while Marx and Engels saw no merit in imagining a persistent tradition of past phenomena informing present struggles, Raymond Williams argued that pre-capitalist values should play a central role in emancipatory processes Perhaps; and regarding Marx, a note of caution: in his last years, as luminous as they are overlooked, he fortunately shifted his stance on issues like these, to some extent, in line with Williams' suggestions.

Indeed, Williams' assertion becomes more comprehensible when applied to the Global South, where, in many aspects, the moral economy has shown great strength in the past—evident in the numerous indigenous communities that have practiced libertarian or liberating ways—and continues to exist in the present. Serge Latouche suggests that the informal economy prevalent in many Southern countries reflects the dichotomy between *a-economy,* referring to intelligent management at the domestic level, and *chrematistics*, which seeks unlimited accumulation. Latouche notes that Ivan Illich used the term *vernacular a-economy* to denote popular practices of survival, which, properly speaking, don't constitute a third sector but rather an extra-sectorial universe. Latouche himself speaks of the existence in the Global South, alongside *capitalist and public sectors*, of a *relational* pole, organized around human values and based on social bonds and communal ownership.

It’s crucial, however, to recognize that categories like these have different meanings or are based on different foundations in Africa, Asia, and America. Primary sociability seems more significant in Africa, where clan systems and family solidarity persist, and where Western modernity has less penetration. It is true, however, that in referring to three *continents* I have used three terms which conceal the fact that behind each of them lie very diverse realities. Certainly, the reality of indigenous communities in the Bolivian highlands is not the same as that prevalent in Lima or Iquique, just as the characteristics of the hill tribes in Southeast Asia differ from those in Saigon or Bangkok.

I'd like to draw three succinct conclusions. First, we should dismiss the notion, dear to Hobsbawm and many Marxist historians, that peasants and artisans are inevitably victims swimming against the current of history. This current has produced such despicable offshoots that one must navigate cautiously when establishing norms in such murky terrain. While this first conclusion does recognize the victimhood of peasants and perhaps artisans, it questions the wisdom of simply accepting that history must, unequivocally, sweep them away.

Secondly, while the values and practices typical of the pre-capitalist moral economy today have a very reduced presence in the rich and urban North of the planet, the same cannot be said for the poorer, often rural South. And thirdly, in line with a thesis adopted by the oft-mentioned Thompson, I suggest that even if envisioning a return to the pre-capitalist world may not make sense —although I'm not sure if an imaginable general collapse might not make us question this— that scenario can still provide alternative horizons for our slim possibilities of action. That is what some of the stories I have tried to bring to the minds of those who have read these pages aspire to.
