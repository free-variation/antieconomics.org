I'd like to return to a topic I discuss in the epilogue: the richness and relevance of pre-capitalist cultures. In one of my books, *Anarquistas de ultramar* \[Overseas anarchists\], published in 2018, I was already drawn to these cultures. I would be deceiving myself, however, if I thought that the selection of texts of the book now in your hands was driven by these cultures. It was only after this book was published that I realized that one of the primary sources of inspiration for these texts was undoubtedly the customs and norms characteristic of the pre-capitalist world. So much so that when I have been asked to sign a copy of this book, I've made it a point to underscore the subtle or overt homage to that world within these pages. This reminds me of Bertolt Brecht's clever remark, that he finally understood his own works after reading the first volume of Marx's *Capital*…

I'm hopeful that the recent release of *The Dawn of Everything: A New History of Humanity* by David Graeber and David Wengrow will finally spark a widespread and positive discussion on the significance of the pre-capitalist world. Despite its many translations and surprising popularity, I suspect that traditional historians may ignore that work, relegating the debate to a niche audience. If this doesn’t occur, the reason would primarily be the growing awareness across societies worldwide of an ecological crisis that exacerbates social tensions, brings us to the brink, and compels us to revisit principles and values that the dominant discourse and accompanying repressive mechanisms have tried to sideline.

A recent perversion of mine is to search everywhere for traces of the pre-capitalist world. For instance, I stumbled upon it while reading Rebecca Solnit's *A Paradise Built in Hell*, which illustrates how common people react to disasters with efficiency, joy, and solidarity. I've also seen it in the mutual aid groups that sprouted across many places in 2020 during the early days of the pandemic. It's striking that many chose to revive the term from Kropotkin's wonderful book, despite most groups being formed by ordinary people, not hyper-aware activists linked to ideologically-informed social movements. Finally, I've noticed this legacy in two geographical locales, Chiapas and Rojava, briefly mentioned in the epilogue. In these places, and others, self-managed cooperative economies have evolved that are wary of state institutions, reject both liberal capitalism and authoritarian socialism, place women at the core of emancipatory projects, strive to preserve ancestral wisdom, and show profound respect for nature and its laws. I believe we would do well to view ourselves in the mirror of these peoples, which is also the mirror in which these antieconomic stories view themselves.
