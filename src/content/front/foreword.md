The purpose of this book is to collect a handful of stories – vignettes, anecdotes, jokes, observations, judgments – which, for better or worse, break with the prevailing economic logic.  These stories prioritize the collective over the individual, avoid equating consumption with wellbeing, value time over money, distrust the supposed virtues of work that is imposed on us, highlight the discrimination suffered by so many women, underline the grim conditions suffered by animals, show interest in practices of solidarity and mutual aid, and remind us of the miseries surrounding our very precarious knowledge of our place in the world.

Forgive me for half-inclining against a discussion of the virtues and defects of the *anecdotal*.  I don’t know whether it was Azaña – a Spanish politician – who said of Ortega – the philosopher–, or Ortega who said of Azaña – more likely the former – that he confused ideas with wisecracks.  It seems to me that if the formula is applied with skill and moderation,  it can’t hurt to encourage this confusion.  A well-crafted story often sheds more light on reality than complex theorizing, despite the risk of losing sight of essential truths.  The seemingly trivial can strengthen our approach to those essences, and enhance our capacity to communicate their meaning.  It can open our eyes and invite us to deepen our understanding of subjects that in other circumstances wouldn’t interest us.  José Saramango was right when he affirmed that “it is true that metaphor presents itself to us as a different illumination of things.  Like a grazing light that illuminates the relief of a painting.  The metaphor is a presentiment of total knowledge”[^1].  If we ask ourselves what we look for in stories, we would likely identify three benefits: they illuminate reality, they make us laugh, and they move us.  If this book manages to achieve the first two objectives, I would be more than satisfied. 

I wish I could tell the reader that the master procedure for elaborating this work began with identifying a handful of topics worth tackling, and then searching for stories that delve deeper into the characteristics and nature of those topics.  Unhappily, things have not conformed to that pattern. Perhaps due to my own lack of skill, the number of stories I managed to gather was small, or at least it seemed that way to me. I had no choice but to start with the texts I had, and shape them into something reasonably coherent.  Even if the process wasn’t ideal, I believe the final outcome is far from insignificant.  The subjects that I mentioned earlier, and others, traverse the pages of this book.  I have preferred to leave the stories just as I received them, despite knowing that some have their own contradictions, e.g. very lucid reflections on corrupt financial-economic schemes alongside a blatant disregard for the rights of animals.  Again, since, instead of matching texts to a preconceived plan, I began with stories for which I have reluctantly sought a plan, the book can be read in whatever hopscotch-like order the reader thinks best.  Little or nothing is lost if the texts are dealt with separately and in the order desired.

I should add that one requirement of the texts included in this selection was that they be brief, which significantly reduced the sample.  I have allowed myself to introduce and comment on them with personal reflections.  Indeed, some of the stories are my own — I suppose it couldn’t be otherwise – though I’m aware of not being at the level of what the writer Menéndez Pelayo has said: “There are many ways of telling an anecdote: reduced to its schematic terms, as in *Discliplina clericalis* or in *El libro de los exemplos*, the anecdote has no aesthetic value at all. The genius of the narrator consists in knowing how to extract from it everything that it truly contains; in reasoning about and motivating the actions of the characters; in seeing them as living figures, not as symbolic abstractions; in noting the picturesque detail, the signifying attitude; in creating a harmonious representations, even though within a very tight framing; in accommodating the dialogue to the character and the character to the intention of the fable; in measuring out with ingenious rhythm the plot of the tale”[^2].

I must clarify the adjective *antieconomic* that I opted to incorporate ahead of the noun *stories* in the title of this work.  I’m fully aware that it would be absurd to demonize the economy as a whole.  There are undoubtedly professionals in the discipline, men and women, operating in heterodox and contestatory registers, whose work aligns closely with those who inspire many of these stories.  I recognize that labeling something "antieconomic" might stir controversy by implying a monolithic, orthodox economy without delving into necessary debates.   It nevertheless seems to me that, even at the cost of inappropriately taking the part for the whole, the most effective way to outline many of the texts here – not all, as some are not directly tied to economic knowledge  – is by identifying a largely unchallenged *official* economy that, rather than giving a punctilious account of reality, strenuously defends an indefensible system: capitalism.  That official economy has found its champions in those who, sold out to power, promoters of an arcane and impenetrable language, and given to endless premeditated manipulations, enjoy an almost limitless echo in the media.  Not to mention, of course, the frequent inanity of a discipline that allows one to conclude that “an economist is an expert who tomorrow will know why something he predicted yesterday did not occur today”[^3], a very unique field in which two individuals can obtain the Nobel Prize after having defended diametrically opposed positions.  Official, academic, and media discourse is often so hilarious that at times, while working on this book, it occurred to me that an anthology of the stories told by the defenders of the existing system might be more fruitful.  It would at least be funnier.
