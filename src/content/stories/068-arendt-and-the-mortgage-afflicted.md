---
number: 68
title: "Arendt and the mortgage-afflicted"
---

My friend S., petite and of fragile health, has dedicated her life to defending just causes. A few years ago, as an activist of the PAH \- the *Plataforma de Afectados por la Hipoteca* \[Platform for People Affected by Mortgages\] in Madrid \- she requested a meeting with the director of a bank branch. The situation at hand was an elderly woman whom the bank wanted to evict from her home. The woman was offered several alternative apartments; some were already occupied by other people, and the remaining one had a hole in the roof through which one could see the stars.

I suppose S. approached the meeting as a sort of bureaucratic obligation. The fact is that during the course of the meeting she took the opportunity to recommend a book by Hannah Arendt titled *Eichmann in Jerusalem* to the inhumane bank branch manager, who was unyielding. Arendt's text is concerned with what she calls the *banality of evil*, and does so through the figure of one of the Nazi hierarchs most directly involved in the Jewish Holocaust. The book reminds us how many people in those circumstances hid behind the *duty of obedience,* and that any one of us could be ensnared by such an infernal machinery.

I assume that S. left the bank with little hope that the elderly woman would ultimately have a decent home. A few days later, to her surprise, my friend received a phone call from the bank branch manager. He had purchased the book, read it, was persuaded by the thesis, and had decided to allocate a decent home to the elderly lady. Arendt would have risen from her grave had she known that one of her works had evidently served a purpose.

Some time after I learned of this story, I met S. one afternoon.  She told me there had been a sequel to the event. One day, she and a friend had entered a bar in a town near Madrid. A gentleman was looking at her intently. When she went to pay, she discovered that this man had paid their bill. She approached him to ask why, and the good man introduced himself as the former director of the bank branch. He had left the bank and opened a bar. I suspect that the cause of such a change was not really the reading of Hannah Arendt, but the persistence of my friend S. Faith moves mountains, and above all, it moves people.
