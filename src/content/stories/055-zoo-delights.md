title: "Zoo delights"
---

I propose to recount two stories concerning our simian brethren.  The first is about what happened with a very well-known albino gorilla named *Copito de Nieve* \[Snowflake\].

According to Salvador Cotelo and Teresa Tristán, *Copito de Nieve* was captured in Africa after his entire family was killed. Purchased in 1966 for a price equivalent to what is now 91 euros, at only two years old, he was placed in a cage at the Barcelona Zoo, in effect receiving a life sentence. As he was a unique specimen, he became the zoo's main attraction. The gorilla's lack of pigmentation, the strength of the Mediterranean sun, and the absence of the forest's shade meant that *Copito de Nieve* would eventually suffer from skin cancer. However, this didn’t seem to bother the zoo's management, which kept him outside despite early signs of skin problems and eye irritation. Although cancer claimed Snowflake's life in 2001, the zoo's business didn’t end there. The animal had been subjected to an ambitious breeding program, intended not to return his offspring to freedom, but rather to exploit new albino gorillas for the sake of profits. Of *Copito*'s twenty offspring, only eight had survived by March 2009\. None of them ever saw the African jungle. All were born, and will die, behind bars.

The second story I mentioned draws from a reflection by Óscar Horta, who invites us to imagine that, for some reason, humans begin to become less intelligent, eventually reaching intellectual capacities similar to those of many chimpanzees today. Suppose also that, in a reverse process, chimpanzees become increasingly intelligent, achieving intellectual abilities similar to those of humans today. And that they decide to hunt humans, confine them in cages, and exploit them for various purposes. How would we judge such behavior?
