title: "Seminars and Mutual Aid"
---

The implementation of the so-called Bologna Process in European universities brought about significant changes in pedagogy and in the allocation of time to various activities: it increased the emphasis on seminars, a feature that certainly existed before, but had previously held lesser importance. When I had to adapt to this change, I decided that the best way to approach the task was to organize debates with the students.

In the heat of these debates, during the first year of the plan’s implementation, I was unpleasantly surprised that the majority of the participants – there were, of course, exceptions – resonated with the idea that societies progress only through the most ferocious competition and ruthless elbowing.  Challenging this perception is no easy task, primarily because it does, undeniably, reflect a significant part of reality. It would be absurd to deny that there is a competitive dimension to human nature. All I could do was emphasize that, fortunately, this complex nature also includes powerful elements of solidarity and cooperation.

On occasion, I ventured to suggest reading a book that I consider wonderful: *Mutual Aid*, by Kropotkin, one of the most famous anarchist thinkers at the turn of the 19th and 20th centuries. Kropotkin was also an internationally renowned scientist. In his book he empirically demonstrates that numerous species of animals thrive through solidarity and cooperation. It’s crucial to emphasize the biological dimension of the argument, which explains \- in my understanding- why, despite the existence of capital, States, and patriarchies, the practice of solidarity has not died.
