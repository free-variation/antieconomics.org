---
number: 79
title: "A man, a fishing rod, a river …"
---

My friend Enrique Falcón is responsible for this epistolary story that portrays how difficult it is to get ahead in a society where the powerful only create obstacles to the unfolding of life.

"Letter from January 21: Today I found a man by the dock who is starving...

Letter from February 8: Remember the man I told you about? Raquel and I have decided to go to the dock once a day and give him some fish to eat.

Letter from February 15: We continue to visit him (...) with daily food, but we fear the day we can't go there and the man at the dock will be left without his fish. He is grateful to us. His cheeks are starting to regain color. He seems a bit stronger. Some nights we've invited him to dinner with the family. He is quite shy.

Letter from March 10: Raquel and I have decided to buy him a fishing rod. We plan to give him a comprehensive and detailed manual on fishing tackle and techniques (...). They say the river is full of fish. We believe that in a short time he’ll be able to fish for himself. He could get food on his own and maybe some money from selling the surplus catch.

Letter from March 23: Problems arise. Learning to fish has not enabled the man at the dock to do without us. He needs a license and some other papers to fish from the river. The permits cost a good amount of money, and he has none to pay. We found out that the river's exploitation is exclusive to the municipality and you can't fish there without the proper paperwork.

Letter from March 25: More problems: the local police caught the man at the dock fishing without a license and now he is detained. The bail (...) is not exactly cheap. We will try to cover it for him. People in the town are saying that he tried to take advantage of the community, that he is a thief and that he deserved it.

Letter from April 29: Another complication, and this one seems serious. Did I tell you that the man at the dock got out of jail and got the necessary fishing permits? Well, they are useless now: the plastic factory in the town, upstream, has polluted the waters and all the fish are dead. Not a single one is left and the sight is desolate. They say there will be no fishing for about ten years or so. The industry will pay an astronomical fine (which it can easily afford), will buy some waste filters, and will continue producing...

Letter from April 30: The man at the dock is starving again."
