number: 47
title: "Lifeboats and *carpe diem*"
---

There are many stories about the voyage and sinking of the Titanic.  It’s hardly a coincidence: to varying degrees they’re all about a marvel of technology that was touted as unsinkable, and yet sank to the bottom of the sea on its very first voyage.  

I now recall two of these anecdotes.  The first begins with the question as to why so many human beings died on board the Titanic. The answer is simple: the lifeboats couldn’t accommodate as many people as were on the ship. I’ve often wondered if this could have been remedied – attributing to the question, of course, a metaphorical significance in relation to the impending collapse. The remedy was clear: to dismantle the ship's luxury cabins and use the wood to build new lifeboats. This proposal has much to do with the need to challenge a widely held view suggesting that we should tackle all problems in such a way that no one loses. In my opinion, this a genuine moral scam: those who have placed us in the current scenario, on the brink of an economic, social, and ecological precipice, must indeed take their losses.

The second anecdote is about the attitude of *carpe diem* and its relation, once again, to discourse on collapse. Legend has it that many of the Titanic’s passengers – perhaps the poorest – aware that the ship was inexorably sinking, went on drinking champagne and dancing a waltz. *Carpe diem* urges us to focus exclusively on what is closest in time and space, openly ignoring everything else. Perhaps it's not a very intelligent option.
