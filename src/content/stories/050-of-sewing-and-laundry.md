number: 50
title: "Of sewing and laundry"
---

In one of her texts, María González Reyes explains the significance of sewing to many women. She describes it in this way: “They gather to sew. Well, actually, they don't gather to sew, they gather for other things. And, incidentally, they sew. If they sew, their husbands don't say anything. If they gather with other women to sew, their husbands don't say anything. Neither does the police. And they sew, incidentally they sew. While they stitch and exchange colored threads, they talk. They talk about their things. Women's things. They talk about economics, about the household, about the world. They talk about politics, about how to reverse unfair situations. They talk about how to take care of those around them. They talk about how to take care of each other. They talk about women's things. And, along the way, they sew.”

María's text reminds me of a tale I've heard a thousand times, encountered in Morocco. It’s about a Spanish non-governmental organization filled with good intentions, aware that the women of a small village had to travel far to the communal laundry area.  The NGO fought to bring water directly to every household. What they didn't realize, of course, was that the distant laundry was the only place where women could talk freely about their own affairs away from the inquisitive gaze of men. Blessed laundry.

The story of the laundry area reminds me of a short tale by Anthony de Mello, titled "The Monkey That Saved a Fish."  Someone asked a monkey why it had taken a fish out of the water and placed it on a tree branch. “I'm saving it from drowning,” replied the monkey... Hell is full of good intentions.
