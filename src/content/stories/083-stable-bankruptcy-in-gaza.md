number: 83
title: "Stable bankruptcy in Gaza"
---

About thirty years ago, I met the Uruguayan ethologist Eduardo Gudynas in Nicaragua. As is often the case, It was an era of hyperinflation in his country. One fine day, I ventured to ask him how they coped with the fact that bread cost ten in the morning and eleven in the afternoon. He replied, with thinly veiled irony, that people in Uruguay lived in a state of stable bankruptcy. It was undoubtedly bankruptcy, but since it unfolded in a predictable manner, the mind ended up adapting to conditions that eventually seemed normal. I suppose we may deduce that the people of Uruguay didn’t see reality as we often do in the North, marked by ups and downs we call crises and booms. Such are the advantages of having a stable bankruptcy.

When writing  a book titled *Collapse*, published in 2016,  I often thought that the concept of collapse, in its everyday use in the Northern countries, has a fairly ethnocentric character. We understand the concept because we assume we’re not yet in it, so we compare our present situation to a possible future. On the other hand, explaining collapse to a girl born in the Gaza Strip seems to me extremely difficult: that girl doesn't have the possibility of comparison, since her entire life has been a genuine collapse. We need to remember that the collapse I'm talking about isn't just something that might happen tomorrow: it's the present reality of hundreds of millions of human beings.
