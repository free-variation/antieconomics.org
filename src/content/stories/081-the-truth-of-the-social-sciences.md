number: 81
title: "The truth of the social sciences"
---

Elias Milojević, my Serbian-Chilean friend who sadly passed away, once told me a story – there are several versions out there – that humorously accounts for the precariousness of much of our knowledge. In a Midwestern American city, known for its technological advancement, there's an indigenous community on the outskirts. This community blends modern developments with ancestral customs.

In August, the leaders of the indigenous community approach the shaman, the religious leader, to ask what the next winter will be like.  The shaman replies confidently that it will be extremely harsh, with extreme temperatures, frequent frosts, torrential rains, and constant snow. The community members immediately start chopping wood like madmen. The conversation repeats in the same terms in September, October, and November, so they continue chopping wood breathlessly. December arrives, and the shaman gives the same response about the severity of the upcoming winter. Once the community leaders have left, however, he begins to doubt and decides to make an appointment at the local weather station.

The director of the station receives him and, when asked about the condition of the upcoming winter, responds, to the shaman's relief, that it will be extremely harsh. The shaman asks how they know this and ventures to suggest that they compare historical data sets from satellites and the records they have had for many years. The station director interrupts him and says, "Not at all, my friend\! We know because the indigenous people have been chopping wood like madmen for five months"... This is how the knowledge of social sciences is often forged, I'm afraid.

Not long ago, I came across a similar story. A person who was impressed by the accuracy of weather forecasts made by a New York institute decided to ask how they were made. The head of the institute explained that it was located in the tallest building in the United States. When asked if the measuring devices worked better because of this, the head said no, that wasn't the reason, and had the person accompany him to the rooftop. Once there, the director of the center showed them the smoke signals emitted by the Dakota indigenous people.
