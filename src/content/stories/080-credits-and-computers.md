title: "Credits and computers"
---

Returning to the story of Uncle Arsenio, I now add a special dimension in connection with the wonderful technologies imposed on us.

John Berger recounts in his book "Pig Earth" that a farmer was offered a loan to acquire a tractor. The cost of the latter was equivalent to the annual yield of ten cows, each producing 2,500 liters of milk – in other words, a fortune. Once the farmer came into possession of the tractor, he was told that to get the most out of it, he needed to acquire various complementary gadgets, forcing him to apply for another loan. These machines in turn required more add-ons, triggering new loans, so the farmer found himself increasingly indebted. In the end, he was forced to sell his business, and the cows, the milk, the tractor, and the auxiliary machines all disappeared.

Berger's story reminds me of another one included in David Anisi's *Cuentos Económicos* \[Economic Tales\].  Anisi tells of a writer who had the healthy habit of capturing his stories on beautiful white paper. One of his admirers suggested that he modernize and use floppy disks to save and send his stories. Our writer bought a computer and spent time learning how to use it. It also took him a lot of effort to transfer the content of his creations onto floppy disks. Meanwhile, he of course stopped writing. When the task was finished, he discovered that the writing program he was using had become outdated and the floppy disks were no longer useful. Our author had to buy a new computer and embark on the tedious process of learning and transferring again, which once more took up his time and prevented him from writing.

As you might imagine, the devices on which he eventually managed to save his stories soon stopped working, so this poor soul had to buy an even more modern computer... After fifteen years of creative drought, the writer earn from a fig seller in the port of Izmir, who, at the end of his work, sat with friends and told the most beautiful tales.
