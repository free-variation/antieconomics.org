title: "The parable of the Mexican fisherman"
---

I tell this story often, and it’s omnipresent in books on degrowth.  In my version, to which I have dedicated a whole book, it’s set in a town of the Mexican coast.

A villager lies half-asleep by the sea. An American tourist approaches and strikes up a conversation with him.  At a certain point the foreigner asks: 'And you, what do you do for a living? What's your job?'. 'I'm a fisherman', replies the Mexican. 'Wow, a very hard job', replies the tourist, who adds: 'I suppose you work many hours every day, right?'. 'Quite a few, yes', responds his interlocutor. 'How many hours do you work on average each day?'. 'I dedicate a couple or three hours to fishing', replies the questioned. 'Two hours? And what do you do with the rest of your time?'. 'I wake up late, fish for a couple of hours, play with my children for a while, take a nap with my wife, and in the evening, I go out to drink a few beers'. 'But, how can you be like this?', the American tourist reacts angrily. 'What do you mean? I don't understand your question'. 'Why don't you work more? If you did, in a couple of years you’d have a bigger boat'. 'And for what?'. 'Later on, you could set up a factory here in the village'. 'And for what?'. 'Over time, you’d set up an office in the capital'. 'And for what?'. 'Years later you’d open branches in the United States and Europe'. 'And for what?'. 'Finally, your company's shares would be listed on the stock exchange and you’d be immensely rich'. 'And all that, for what?', inquires the Mexican. 'Well' \- replies the tourist \-, 'when you’re, I don't know, 65 or 70 years old, you can retire peacefully and come to live here in this village, to wake up late, fish for a couple of hours, play with your grandchildren, take a nap with your wife, and go out in the evening to drink a few beers'.

I want to highlight two dimensions to this tale. Firstly, the Mexican protagonist isn't a revolutionary, nor does he advocate for an alternate social order. His request is simple: to be left alone. This desire comes from a clear disdain for the chaos and misery of capitalist frenzy. Second, the story contains many omissions. Suffice it to say that it fails to tell us how many hours the Mexican protagonist's wife worked...
