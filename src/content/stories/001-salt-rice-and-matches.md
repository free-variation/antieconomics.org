number: 1
title: "Salt, rice, and matches"
---

Derzu Uzala is the protagonist of a highly celebrated book in which Vladimir Arseniev narrates the life of a nomadic hunter living in the easternmost lands of Siberia.  The book was successfully adapted into a film by Akira Kurosawa.

Arseniev writes that one day, after waking up from a nap, he found that Dersu had collected firewood and tree bark, and placed them in a cabin.  Arseniev thought that the hunter intended to burn down the cabin, and tried to convince Dersu to not do so.  The hunter, without saying a word, asked for a little salt and a handful of rice while wrapping some matches in bark, along with the salt and the rice.  He hung the two packets on a wall in the hut and prepared to leave.  When Arseniev asked him if he planned to return soon, Derzu shook his head and indicated that someday someone would come to the cabin and would be grateful to find dry wood, matches, and a little food.  Derzu was thinking of a stranger he would never see, who, in turn, would never know whom to thank for the fire and the food.

This story reminds me of another, heard so many times, of how people in the Mediterranean – and many other places – had, and perhaps today still have, the custom of planting slowly-maturing trees.  Knowing very well that they would never benefit from those trees, and evidently thinking of future generations. In the eyes of those of us who received a Western education, it’s hard to understand that trees have spirits.  And yet, as Norbert S. Hill has emphasized, for many indigenous communities, the universe is alive.  When we see members of these communities talking to trees, we’re not witnessing any sort of mental disorder.

According to a saying of the indigenous communities of North America, each generation must secure the future of the next seven.  How far has our ignominy gone, that, in the most enlightened version of ourselves, we merely ask that each generation pass on to the next the same natural capital it inherited from the previous.  In the most enlightened version, I repeat.
