title: "Nuclear salvation"
---

I don't know the author of the phrase, but it’s widely acclaimed, and goes roughly like this: "I am a firm supporter of the complete abolition of the death penalty, except in the case of architects." I allow myself to replace “architects” with radio and television commentators. You know the ones: those individuals, the *todólogos* \[know-it-alls; literally, everything-ologists\], who, in their most generous moments, begin their remarks by confessing they know nothing about the topic they are about to cover, only to immediately demonstrate that, for once, they were entirely right and indeed knew nothing.

A few years ago, while watching a discussion on a television channel, I was amazed to see one of the usual *todólogos* – a person accustomed to abusing the Spanish language, fond of nebulous arguments, and always servile to the powerful – appear to be quite inspired. I'm not being ironic: he produced what I considered an extremely lucid, serious, and meticulous reflection on the delicate panorama we face due to the exhaustion of all the energy raw materials we use today. When he finished his intervention, I understood what was happening: this sharp reflection was aimed expressly at showing that there was only one solution. It's easy to guess what that was: nuclear energy. I don't think I'm wrong in suggesting that this gentleman had dined the night before with a press officer of a nuclear industry company, who had instructed him on what to argue to justify the conclusion that this form of energy was the only viable way out.

Let’s not let ourselves be deceived.  According to the commonly cited data, there is enough uranium on the planet for 35 to 40 years. Those who want to make nuclear energy our lifeline claim that we need to at least triple the current number of nuclear power plants. The arithmetic is simple: we'll have uranium for 12 to 15 years. No one knows what to do with the waste generated by these plants, which is yet another awful legacy for future generations. The construction of these plants is very costly in terms of climate change, the energy they produce is universally expensive and almost always requires costly public subsidies. Not to mention the safety issues of the plants, a debate intensified by the Japanese accident some years ago. Our *todólogo* friend should eat less, read more, and, above all, talk to different people.
