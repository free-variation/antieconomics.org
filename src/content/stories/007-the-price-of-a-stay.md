number: 7
title: "The price of a stay"
---

Thanks to Fernando Esteve, I have come across a story told by Bruce Chatwin in his book *In Patagonia*. A customer spent the night in a hotel in Río Pico (Argentina) run "by a Jewish family that had no basic notion of what profit was".  As the customer prepared to pay the next morning, this dialogue took place: "'How much do I owe you for the room?'. 'Nothing. If you hadn't slept in it, no one would have.' 'And how much do I owe you for dinner?'. 'Nothing. How could we have known you were coming? We cooked for ourselves.' 'So, how much do I owe you for the wine?'. 'Nothing. We always serve wine to guests.' 'And what about the *mate*?'. 'Nobody pays for *mate.*' 'What can I pay for, then? Only bread and coffee are left.' 'I can't charge you for the bread, but coffee with milk is a *gringo* thing and I'll make you pay for it.'"

Someone will say that a business, let alone an economy in general, cannot function according to practices like those that Chatwin ascribes to this very singular Jewish family – we do well, of course, to break xenophobic prejudices in this regard. And yet, there’s strong evidence that from time immemorial many human societies have conformed to these practices. What's more, they’ve often justified them with the noble principle of reciprocity. This leads me to wonder: how much could we benefit if we embraced a genuine de-marketization of our interactions and relationships? Like the hotel owners, who clearly gave importance to realities other than money.
