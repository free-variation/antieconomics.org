number: 14
title: "The intricacies of business communication"
---

We don’t pay enough attention to business communication – it’s a domain full of riches. A few years ago I boarded a flight in Madrid, on my way to Galicia. The plane left with a three-hour delay, during which we were forced to sit, immobile, in the cabin. At a certain point, the flight attendant, who clearly had a sense of humor, addressed the passengers. He reported that on the previous flight, to explain a delay, they had suggested that the plane had been abducted by aliens, adding that sadly it wasn’t true.  Because, he added, categorically, the explanation for the delay was different: the company overexploited the planes and did the same to its employees.

The flight attendant's words fell like a bombshell among the passengers. In half a minute he managed to defuse what could’ve become a revolt. Knowing that the airline’s employees were in a worse situation than ours, it was only logical to keep quiet. My takeaway was that we had just witnessed a complex and somewhat risky example of corporate communication, that even at the risk of tarnishing the airline's image, effectively defused a tense situation.  Of course, other interpretations were possible, including the idea that the flight attendant had been fired that day and was seeking revenge with his announcement. But that seems rather unlikely.

I recall a second instance, which, perhaps, points to the logic of sabotage. Some time ago, I read a newspaper advertisement for a bank. The premise of the advertisement was genuine nonsense. By investing half a million euros to an investment fund, anyone willing to take such a significant risk would be entitled to a trip to London for two to attend a Premier League match. The offer was clearly ridiculous: for someone with half a million euros, a plane ticket, a night in a hotel, and a ticket to a football match would hardly trigger interest in the bank. Even if it was for two people.

But what really drew my eyes to the advertisement was the specific wording of the main phrase, which read: 'For *only* half a million euros...'. The use of the word 'only' in this context only made sense by imagining the figure of someone who, in an advertising studio, chose to express revenge through irony.  Sabotage, in short. I chose to keep my half a million euros in another bank.
