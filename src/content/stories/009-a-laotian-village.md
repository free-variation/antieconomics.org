number: 9
title: "A Laotian village"
---

International financial organizations are irritated by life. The relationship between the two is vividly illustrated by a story set in Laos, recounted by Pierre Rabhi in one of his books.

A villager recounts life in a Laotian village, home to about two hundred people, located next to a forest and on the banks of a river. The rice they cultivated was their main food. The houses were built with native materials, and the lands were suited to the capabilities of the community’s buffaloes. The harvest was stored in granaries located along the road bordering the village. The cereals, fruits, and legumes that were consumed were supplemented by the fish provided by the river. Rabhi recalls that the logic of mutual support and reciprocity was dominant, exemplified by practices of collective fishing, which allowed for the accumulation of dried fish.

The community took care of widows, the elderly, those without relatives, and, in general, people facing difficulties. A bonze ensured social harmony and acted as a judge. The general viewpoint, inspired by Buddhism, viewed everything as sacred. Only one negative element stood out:  the habitual clearing of land to expand cultivation areas, which was full-fledged aggression against the natural environment. And one wonders about their treatment of animals.

One fine day, an expert from the World Bank visited the village to study its way of life. After his stay, he wrote a report back to the World Bank, remarking that the Laotian community, although deserving of sympathy, couldn’t develop or grow because it spent too much time on unproductive activities. Rabhi's conclusion is straightforward: although the community clearly met the needs of its members, it didn’t generate the type of wealth that the powerful want . It refused to exchange real wealth, the genuine kind, for the dollars of the dominant pseudo-economy.
