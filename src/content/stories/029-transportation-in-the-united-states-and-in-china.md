number: 29
title: "Transportation in the United States and in China"
---

Many years ago, I came across an article written by a Nobel Prize-winning economist, which I've since lost track of. In this piece, Wassily Leontieff made a comparison between the transportation systems of the United States and China. It's important to note that, contrary to appearances, the author's intention was not to evaluate two macro-economic systems, e.g. liberal capitalism versus whatever system existed in China at the time. We could substitute the name of China with Thailand or Myanmar, and I suspect the argument would remain largely unchanged.

The Nobel laureate began by pointing out that the United States has the most developed transportation system in the world. The country had a vast network of highways and freeways, the highest number of cars per inhabitant, and the highest levels of gasoline consumption on the planet. However, when it came to assessing how this hyper-developed transportation system met daily needs, its problems became apparent. The average inhabitant of the United States lived 55 minutes by car from their workplace, had to get up very early each morning, often found themselves engulfed in gigantic traffic jams that were damaging to their nerves and to the environment, only to frequently end up arriving late to work.

At that time – I am speaking, it should be remembered, of several decades ago – China barely made an appearance in international statistical yearbooks. There were neither highways nor freeways in the country, the number of privately owned cars was very low, and the per capita consumption of gasoline was laughable. And yet, the average inhabitant lived five minutes by bicycle from their workplace, could sleep an hour more each morning than their American counterpart, and never found themselves caught in formidable traffic jams that damaged their nerves (and the environment) – and usually arrived on time to work.

Leontieff wondered, of course, which of these two transportation systems was objectively more developed.  He made it clear that he didn't want to overlook the fact that most of the Chinese population probably didn't consume enough calories to lead a dignified life. That must be said.
