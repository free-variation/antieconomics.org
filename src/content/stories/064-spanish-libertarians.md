title: "Spanish libertarians"
---

I have often thought – and I will return to this in the epilogue of this book – that the fascination that we continue to feel for pre-1939 Spanish anarchism is rooted in a specific reality.  In the anarchist world, two distinct yet complementary impulses spontaneously converged, one of a pre-capitalist character and the other of an anti-capitalist nature.

The first was linked to the rural origins of many of these individuals, and was materialized in the non-ideological and experiential preservation of collective work practices and behaviors, rooted in solidarity and mutual support. The second impulse was caused by anarchist ideology, which, proudly anti-capitalist, gave impetus to many of the collective and solidarity-oriented elements that the pre-capitalist impulse provided.

The combination of these two elements had many fortunate consequences. For example, it enabled the suggestive experiment of the collectivizations that took shape during the Spanish Civil War. They emphatically demonstrated that it’s possible to manage a complex society without relying on entrepreneurs, foremen, and bureaucrats. But it also made possible a strike in which a significant part of the proletariat of Zaragoza showed their solidarity with a German communist leader imprisoned by the Nazis. Note that I'm not talking about a strike motivated by labor issues. It wasn't about wage increases or demands for reduced working hours.  The reduction of working hours was, however, behind the demands for respect of religious holidays made during the civil war by many anarcho-syndicalist workers, who understood with unyielding rigor that a revolution that did not entail a reduction in working hours was unimaginable. I suspect that behind this lay the lingering influence of many codes characteristic of pre-capitalist societies.
