title: "Planned obsolescence"
---

Planned obsolescence is by no means a new phenomenon. A well-known example will suffice. I have in mind a light bulb, conceived by Adolphe Chaillet and manufactured by the Shelby Electric Company in 1895, which is still functioning today. Major electrical companies quickly agreed to end such unprofitable economic nonsense, and began producing light bulbs that stopped working after a short time. They also prevented Narva light bulbs, produced in East Germany and significantly more durable than those manufactured by the giants of the capitalist world, from entering the markets of Western countries.

The phenomenon is apparent everywhere. A long time ago a formula was found that made women's stockings almost unbreakable. Again, bad for business. We know that most printers are programmed to stop working after just a few printed sheets. It’s obvious that decades ago our appliances lasted longer, much longer.

Let’s not forget that planned obsolescence also has other more subtle manifestations. Latouche points to a clearly induced form of behavior that surely benefits manufacturers and distributors: people drive to the supermarket once a week, fill their cart with the latest deals, fill the refrigerator with the purchased items, heat the food in the microwave and, time and again, discover that many of those products have passed their expiration date....
