number: 12
title: "How we die"
---

I often come across texts that call attention to something important, very important, which we nevertheless overlook. In the rich North, over the past few decades, the manner of dying has changed. Specifically, I'm referring to deaths that don't occur abruptly or unexpectedly, but rather as the culmination of an inevitable process, driven either by illness or advanced age.

In times past, it was common to die at home. This was certainly true in rural areas, but also in cities. People usually died surrounded by the attention and affection of their loved ones. The technological paraphernalia to which we are accustomed today didn’t exist. Nor did the sometimes harmful effects of hospitals and their regulations. This older way of dying served to alleviate both physical pain and pain linked to death itself and its rosary of grief and loneliness.  It’s hardly surprising that even today, many elderly people prefer to die at home. They have their reasons.

It’s well known that today, what I have described is uncommon. We die in an unfamiliar environment, a hospital, often in a closed and monitored enclosure far removed from our loved ones, surrounded by devices as strange as they are annoying, and often subject to a myriad of strict legal rules. Paradoxically, this environment doesn't always alleviate physical pain, even as it seems to increase the pain of loss.

I recognize that in comparing the old and the new, there’s an important fact: nowadays we live many more years than did our ancestors who died in their homes. However, even this seemingly clear advantage is not without controversy. Some believe we are pushing against our biological limits, with not inconsiderable negative consequences. This raises a fundamental question: Are we actually happy during these additional years we've gained?
