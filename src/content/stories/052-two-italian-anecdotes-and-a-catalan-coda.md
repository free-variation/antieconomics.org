title: "Two Italian anecdotes, and a Catalan coda"
---

In September 2011, I traveled to Turin, in northern Italy, to participate in a debate about the *15 de Mayo* movement. That short trip left me with two anecdotes that draw from eternal debates about the public sphere, its grandeur, and its miseries.

The first took the form of a conversation between a French colleague and an Italian one, and it wasn't a joke. The French friend said he couldn’t understand how so many Italians could admire or vote for a despicable character like Berlusconi, who, he added, openly hired prostitutes for his nightly parties. The Italian didn’t deny the main point: he readily admitted that many Italians admired Berlusconi, or at least voted for him.  But he then suggested that this admiration might be rooted in something more perverse than what his interlocutor's indignation suggested. He explained that many admired Berlusconi because he paid for prostitutes *with public money*, which – obviously – places us on different terrain.

For my second anecdote, I remember that on the walls of the streets in the center of Turin, there were very eye-catching advertising posters for a local satirical magazine. The main headline read: "The State and the Mafia are splitting up." Following this was a statement from a mafia boss, which stated simply: "They were ruining our reputation." Just like real life.

If you're looking for a response to such miseries, I can think of no better one given by Gustavo Duch. He tells of José Ramón González visiting one of the people I most admire, Bishop Pere Casaldàliga, in Mato Grosso in Brazil, and asking him how to fight hunger. Pere replied something along these lines: "Look, hunger doesn’t wait. The hungry need to be fed. Then comes teaching them to fish. But, above all, above all else, they must know that the river is theirs."
