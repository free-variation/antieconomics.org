title: "Diogenes’ lentils"
---

Anthony de Mello recounts in one of his texts that one day the philosopher Diogenes was eating lentils for dinner.  Another philosopher, Aristippus, who lived well because he consistently flattered the king time and again, said to Diogenes: 'If you would learn to submit to the king, you would not have to eat those miserable lentils, Diogenes'. The latter replied very quickly: 'If you, Aristippus, had learned, as you should, to eat lentils, you would not have to flatter the king'.

This story reminds me of how many bonds we end up accepting in order to access goods and privileges that in fact contribute nothing substantial to our lives. We would gain much if we could distance ourselves from the voluntary servitude discussed by La Boétie and, likewise, from such dependencies. A few weeks ago, during a public event, an elderly lady said something that seemed very sensible to me. She argued that personal austerity, refraining from unnecessary spending, the intention to work less and in a field we enjoy, the desire to enhance our personal relationships, and the resolve to rebel, should all be our guides regardless of what happens around us – whether the ecological crisis worsens or not, whether collapse is imminent or not, and whether international summits serve any purpose or not.

I often come across people who, even if in good faith, believe that those of us advocating  for practices of degrowth would be incapable of maintaining a complex society like the present one, and of satisfying all our needs.  Like Diogenes, I can only respond that our goal isn’t at all to maintain such a complex society, and that we reserve the right to question the value of these so-called needs. Lentils, well cooked, are very tasty. Diogenes knew this perfectly well.
