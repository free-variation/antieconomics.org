title: "Indigenous peoples and self-restraint"
---

Here is a collection of texts that emerged from indigenous communities, representing a genuine claim to the land, a claim clearly opposed to the miseries characterizing the Western man's relationship with it. The first notion that I believe inspires these texts is *self-restraint*.

Norbert S. Hill reminds us of the behavior of many of the indigenous peoples of North America: "When we Indians kill meat, we eat it. When we dig roots, we make small holes. When we build houses, we also make small holes. When we burn grass, we don’t ruin the burned earth. We shake the acorns and pine cones. We don’t cut down trees. We only use dead wood. But the white man plows the ground, cuts down trees, kills everything. The white man doesn’t pay attention. How can the spirit of the land and the white man get along?"

I have emphasized elsewhere that Eugene P. Odum has noted, with provocative intent, how some Indian cultures located in what we now call the United States show characteristics of a “mature” ecosystem: “protection as against production, stability as against growth, quality as against quantity. In Pueblo societies a kind of ultimate democracy is practiced. Plants and animals are also people, and, through certain rituals and dances, are given a place and a voice in the political discussions of the humans: They are ‘*represented*.’”

Pierre Clastres has written that if primitive humans didn’t maximize the efficiency of their activity, it wasn’t because they didn’t know how, but rather because they didn’t want to: they didn’t aspire to merely profit from such activities. Strikingly, in the languages of many indigenous communities in the Amazon, there is no word for "work". Time is spent laughing, talking, celebrating. When a cabin needs to be built, it’s built. When hunting is needed for food, they hunt. But these activities aren’t unnecessarily prolonged.
