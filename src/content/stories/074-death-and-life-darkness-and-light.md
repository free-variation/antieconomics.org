---
number: 74
title: "Death and life, darkness and light"
---

There are two texts by María González Reyes that faithfully portray, for women, death and darkness – the one \-, and life and light – the other. The first is about women who work as live-in employees in houses that aren’t theirs. “There are many hard things about our job. Some are small details, like not drying your body after a shower with your own towel, or with a friend's towel who lent you a room to live. You dry off with the towel of someone who pays you a salary on which you couldn’t survive a month in the city. You always dry off with the work towel, at work, because you live at work. I know it seems trivial to you, drying your face with a towel that belongs to the person who pays you a salary they would never accept for themselves. It touches your skin. It takes away the water. You can't have a life project if you live in a house that’s your job. A house full of towels that are piled in the cupboards. One day. Another day. The same towel in the same cupboard in the same house. The days. And I’m that face without its own towel to dry off. I’m that face that looks into a round mirror after wiping the water from the face. There’s no intimacy for live-in workers. There’s loneliness, in the smallest room of the house, in the most secluded area. There’s loneliness. There’s no life project because there are no life projects without other people. There’s no joint project for two. There’s no partner. There’s no possibility of children. There’s no everyday life beyond who pays you. A miserable salary. There’s no sex with another person if you live at your work. Live-in workers also like sex. I don't know if you’d thought about that.”

The second text I mentioned is about a woman who spoke up at a public event. "I'm going to tell you," she said, "what my grandmother used to say. My grandmother spoke of the economy of open hands. She would look at me and say: ‘Go fetch me an avocado in the yard. But count with your hand. One avocado has to be for the earth, so another avocado can grow. The second is for the birds, so they can also continue living. The third is for the neighbors, in case any of them needs it. The fourth is for us to cook our recipe. And the fifth is for the one who comes.’" The grandmother – María adds – explained in a very graphic way how life should be at the center.
