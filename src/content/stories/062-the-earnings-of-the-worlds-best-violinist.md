title: "The earnings of the world’s best violinist"
---

A story that reminds me a little of the bookstore anecdote is a juicy tale by Argentine writer César Aira. An immensely wealthy woman who, wanting to add prestige to a dinner party, decided to entertain her guests with violin music and set out to hire the world's best violinist for the occasion, whom she assured she could afford.

Someone suggested the name of the violinist, reputed to be the finest in the world – his name and surname were unmistakably German – and the dinner hostess promptly called him. The violinist, presumably quite matter-of-fact, said he didn't perform at private events as his fees were very high and he doubted anyone could meet them. The lady, undeterred, asked how much he charged, and the violinist quoted a supposedly unreachable figure of ten thousand dollars. "No problem. I'll expect you tonight," the wealthy woman replied, but she immediately added a condition: "You will dine in the kitchen with the servants and under no circumstances may you speak to my guests." "In that case," the musician retorted, "my fee changes." "No problem," the dinner hostess agreed. "Tell me how much it is." "Two thousand dollars," the violinist stated.

While I understand this story often appears in psychology books, I find it fits better in textbooks on sociology or, even better, ethics. The violinist preferred the company and conversation of the servants and was therefore willing to charge much less. Or even, why not, play for free.
