number: 35
title: "Sobriety and generations"
---

While I'm certain that many of our concepts have a generational dimension, we seldom pay attention to this aspect. It seems clear to me that terms like "voluntary simplicity" and "sobriety" hold different meanings for someone born in 1940, someone born in 1970, and for a twenty-year-old today.

Let me share two experiences that support this conclusion. The first relates to a figure that has nearly vanished from Spanish trains: an elderly man or woman who begins to eat in the carriage, taking out a sandwich and a piece of fruit. The reverence with which they handled these foods could only be explained by a scarcity they must have experienced in their life. Those of us born later have a cooler, and I believe, less intelligent relationship with food: we take for granted that it's always there, readily available to us. This is a serious mistake, especially considering what might be looming on the horizon.

Secondly, every year until recently, I would receive a phone call in Madrid from a group of elderly women, inquiring about my availability to give a talk. About a decade ago, they asked what I wanted to discuss, and I chose to talk about degrowth. On the day of my speech, however, I didn't have time to prepare, which usually meant taking a moment to consider the audience and, if necessary, reorienting arguments, examples, or anecdotes. So, I spoke to these ladies about degrowth as I usually do, but I did so fearing they might not understand me, potentially leading to a conflictual situation.

That wasn't the case. I think they quickly grasped what the degrowth perspective entails. Perhaps they looked back on a difficult time in their youth – the darkest and most obscurantist days of Franco's regime –, remembered what sobriety and voluntary simplicity meant back then, and concluded that these values were indeed worthwhile. I'm thinking of a time when a sweater had to last a decade and throwing away a crust of bread was unthinkable. These good women retained in their minds a pristine space that, when appropriately stimulated, could yield pleasant surprises.
