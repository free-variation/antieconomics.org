number: 100
title: "The “we” of Marcos"
---

I don't know which word is most used in Spanish. When speaking, and especially at the beginning of sentences, I suspect it is *yo* \[I\]. Although some might argue, quite reasonably, that this is logical, I prefer to view mountains and seas from a different perspective and suggest that "yo" unfortunately displaces *nosotros* or *nosotras* \[we, masculine and feminine\], and that it often reflects the traits of societies burdened by a regrettable individualism. This text by Subcomandante Marcos gives us food for thought on this matter.

"According to our cultural tradition, the world was created by several gods. Gods who were very much into dancing, very explosive \- as we also say \- who didn't do a complete job. They left things hanging, or things that were done poorly. One of these was that they did not make all men and women upright, that is, with a good heart. Instead, some governor or some president of the country ended up with an evil soul and a crooked heart. When the gods realized this injustice, that there were men and women living at the expense of others, they wanted to help the “corn people” – the indigenous peoples of this country. And to help them, they removed a word: they removed the 'I.' In indigenous communities, in those of Mayan roots and in many peoples of this country, the word 'I' does not exist. In its place, 'we' is used. In our Mayan languages, it's 'tic.' This 'tic' ending, which refers to the collective or community, is repeated over and over again. And nowhere does 'I' appear. 'We are not afraid to die fighting,' we say. We never speak in singular. The 'tic,' which repeats itself over and over in our languages, is like the tick-tock of the clock we want to carry, to be part of this country, without being a shame for it, an affront or a reason for mockery or charity."
