title: "Happiness in the Paleolithic"
---

I have already pointed out that when the English Luddites began destroying machines during the first Industrial Revolution at the beginning of the 19th century, they faced unanimous condemnation. This criticism emphasized that the machines would be beneficial for everyone as they would shorten the workday and multiply wealth. However, nothing has confirmed such a claim: today, globally, those who have jobs are often forced to work more and more, often under worse conditions.

Many societies of the past don’t conform to the image of humans fighting desperately and working to exhaustion to survive. There was often a certain natural abundance, time dedicated to work was less, and the pressures exerted by life were reasonably bearable. The writings of an American anthropologist, Marshall Sahlins, suggest that in the Paleolithic era, daily chores required about the same amount of time as today's commute to work. For a more recent example, according to one estimate members of the peasantry in medieval Europe worked an average of 1,620 hours annually, while today a wage earner in the United States has to devote around 2,000 hours a year to work.

Work appears to have become more intense and demanding over the centuries, and mechanisms of control and pressure have intensified. While more and more people are unemployed, the general trend in affluent societies is, again, to *increase* the number of working hours. This is yet another of the paradoxes, to put it mildly, of contemporary capitalism.
