number: 4
title: "Work to live, not trade"
---

In an acclaimed book, *The Innocent Anthropologist*, Nigel Barley recounts an experience he had among the inhabitants of the Dowayo community in Cameroon.  These people knew well that the millet they cultivated fetched high prices in the cities of the nation.  However, the prospect of selling their millet in the cities didn’t appeal to them, because the markets were controlled by Fulani merchants who, says Barley, aimed for profits of one to two hundred percent in their transactions.  Since these merchants were also responsible for millet transportation, it was clear to the Dowayo that the payment they would receive for their crops would be minimal at best. Consequently, rather than participate in a system rife with greed and exploitation, the Dowayo community preferred to grow just enough to meet their needs, turning their backs on the seemingly lucrative but exploitative urban market.  

This choice obviously had a beneficial consequence: one could work many fewer hours than otherwise required to satisfy the urban demand for millet. The same was true of much of the European peasantry, in the Middle Ages.  Like the Dowayo, they too were not driven by the urge to accumulate for sale but were satisfied with fulfilling their basic needs, or just a bit more.

But the case of the Dowayo community also brings to mind the notably low productivity that was characteristic, from at least the 1950s, in Soviet-style systems. This lack of productivity was seemingly a form of working-class resistance against the authoritarianism and dysfunction inherent in those systems.  The choice to work more or less should be a free one – something that was not the case in those systems and is not in current capitalist societies.  How many among us wouldn’t prefer to significantly reduce the working day, even at the cost of earning less?  But the rules of the game prevent it, or at least make it difficult.
