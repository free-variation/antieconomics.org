number: 46
title: "Technology and traps"
---

The most delicate question I have been asked was at a public event years ago, in Vigo, Galicia.  During my talk, I had expressed the need to critically analyze the nature of the technologies that the system gives us, and concluded that these technologies are not as neutral as they might seem.

A local man took to the floor to explain that he was alive thanks to the very latest technology, which had made possible a delicate surgical operation, and asked if I was opposed to that technology. My response – which I think was as reasonable as it was unimaginative – was that I had nothing against the technology in principle, but, to form a definite judgment, at least two important questions had to be asked. The first was about the social nature of that technology, lest it only benefit, say, 5 percent of the planetary population, in which case we would of course have a problem. The second concerned the ecological-energetic character of the new technology, whether its adoption today hinders access to it for future generations. Had I been more astute, I would’ve added that one ought to scrutinize the political dimensions of the technology, lest its adoption depend on the most aberrant hierarchy.

I should add that the significance of debates about technology vary depending on context.  At the beginning of the 19th century, a very lucid movement emerged in England, Luddism, which sought to destroy many of the technologies of the first industrial revolution. Even then, the moneyed classes told the working class the same thing we hear today: these technologies will allow for a reduction in working hours and significantly increase your well-being. Then, as now, they lied.
