# Foreword to the English edition

I'd like to return to a topic I discuss in the epilogue: the richness and relevance of pre-capitalist cultures. In one of my books, *Anarquistas de ultramar* \[Overseas anarchists\], published in 2018, I was already drawn to these cultures. I would be deceiving myself, however, if I thought that the selection of texts of the book now in your hands was driven by these cultures. It was only after this book was published that I realized that one of the primary sources of inspiration for these texts was undoubtedly the customs and norms characteristic of the pre-capitalist world. So much so that when I have been asked to sign a copy of this book, I've made it a point to underscore the subtle or overt homage to that world within these pages. This reminds me of Bertolt Brecht's clever remark, that he finally understood his own works after reading the first volume of Marx's *Capital*…

I'm hopeful that the recent release of *The Dawn of Everything: A New History of Humanity* by David Graeber and David Wengrow will finally spark a widespread and positive discussion on the significance of the pre-capitalist world. Despite its many translations and surprising popularity, I suspect that traditional historians may ignore that work, relegating the debate to a niche audience. If this doesn’t occur, the reason would primarily be the growing awareness across societies worldwide of an ecological crisis that exacerbates social tensions, brings us to the brink, and compels us to revisit principles and values that the dominant discourse and accompanying repressive mechanisms have tried to sideline.

A recent perversion of mine is to search everywhere for traces of the pre-capitalist world. For instance, I stumbled upon it while reading Rebecca Solnit's *A Paradise Built in Hell*, which illustrates how common people react to disasters with efficiency, joy, and solidarity. I've also seen it in the mutual aid groups that sprouted across many places in 2020 during the early days of the pandemic. It's striking that many chose to revive the term from Kropotkin's wonderful book, despite most groups being formed by ordinary people, not hyper-aware activists linked to ideologically-informed social movements. Finally, I've noticed this legacy in two geographical locales, Chiapas and Rojava, briefly mentioned in the epilogue. In these places, and others, self-managed cooperative economies have evolved that are wary of state institutions, reject both liberal capitalism and authoritarian socialism, place women at the core of emancipatory projects, strive to preserve ancestral wisdom, and show profound respect for nature and its laws. I believe we would do well to view ourselves in the mirror of these peoples, which is also the mirror in which these antieconomic stories view themselves.

# Foreword

The purpose of this book is to collect a handful of stories – vignettes, anecdotes, jokes, observations, judgments – which, for better or worse, break with the prevailing economic logic.  These stories prioritize the collective over the individual, avoid equating consumption with wellbeing, value time over money, distrust the supposed virtues of work that is imposed on us, highlight the discrimination suffered by so many women, underline the grim conditions suffered by animals, show interest in practices of solidarity and mutual aid, and remind us of the miseries surrounding our very precarious knowledge of our place in the world.

Forgive me for half-inclining against a discussion of the virtues and defects of the *anecdotal*.  I don’t know whether it was Azaña – a Spanish politician – who said of Ortega – the philosopher–, or Ortega who said of Azaña – more likely the former – that he confused ideas with wisecracks.  It seems to me that if the formula is applied with skill and moderation,  it can’t hurt to encourage this confusion.  A well-crafted story often sheds more light on reality than complex theorizing, despite the risk of losing sight of essential truths.  The seemingly trivial can strengthen our approach to those essences, and enhance our capacity to communicate their meaning.  It can open our eyes and invite us to deepen our understanding of subjects that in other circumstances wouldn’t interest us.  José Saramango was right when he affirmed that “it is true that metaphor presents itself to us as a different illumination of things.  Like a grazing light that illuminates the relief of a painting.  The metaphor is a presentiment of total knowledge”[^1].  If we ask ourselves what we look for in stories, we would likely identify three benefits: they illuminate reality, they make us laugh, and they move us.  If this book manages to achieve the first two objectives, I would be more than satisfied. 

I wish I could tell the reader that the master procedure for elaborating this work began with identifying a handful of topics worth tackling, and then searching for stories that delve deeper into the characteristics and nature of those topics.  Unhappily, things have not conformed to that pattern. Perhaps due to my own lack of skill, the number of stories I managed to gather was small, or at least it seemed that way to me. I had no choice but to start with the texts I had, and shape them into something reasonably coherent.  Even if the process wasn’t ideal, I believe the final outcome is far from insignificant.  The subjects that I mentioned earlier, and others, traverse the pages of this book.  I have preferred to leave the stories just as I received them, despite knowing that some have their own contradictions, e.g. very lucid reflections on corrupt financial-economic schemes alongside a blatant disregard for the rights of animals.  Again, since, instead of matching texts to a preconceived plan, I began with stories for which I have reluctantly sought a plan, the book can be read in whatever hopscotch-like order the reader thinks best.  Little or nothing is lost if the texts are dealt with separately and in the order desired.

I should add that one requirement of the texts included in this selection was that they be brief, which significantly reduced the sample.  I have allowed myself to introduce and comment on them with personal reflections.  Indeed, some of the stories are my own — I suppose it couldn’t be otherwise – though I’m aware of not being at the level of what the writer Menéndez Pelayo has said: “There are many ways of telling an anecdote: reduced to its schematic terms, as in *Discliplina clericalis* or in *El libro de los exemplos*, the anecdote has no aesthetic value at all. The genius of the narrator consists in knowing how to extract from it everything that it truly contains; in reasoning about and motivating the actions of the characters; in seeing them as living figures, not as symbolic abstractions; in noting the picturesque detail, the signifying attitude; in creating a harmonious representations, even though within a very tight framing; in accommodating the dialogue to the character and the character to the intention of the fable; in measuring out with ingenious rhythm the plot of the tale”[^2].

I must clarify the adjective *antieconomic* that I opted to incorporate ahead of the noun *stories* in the title of this work.  I’m fully aware that it would be absurd to demonize the economy as a whole.  There are undoubtedly professionals in the discipline, men and women, operating in heterodox and contestatory registers, whose work aligns closely with those who inspire many of these stories.  I recognize that labeling something "antieconomic" might stir controversy by implying a monolithic, orthodox economy without delving into necessary debates.   It nevertheless seems to me that, even at the cost of inappropriately taking the part for the whole, the most effective way to outline many of the texts here – not all, as some are not directly tied to economic knowledge  – is by identifying a largely unchallenged *official* economy that, rather than giving a punctilious account of reality, strenuously defends an indefensible system: capitalism.  That official economy has found its champions in those who, sold out to power, promoters of an arcane and impenetrable language, and given to endless premeditated manipulations, enjoy an almost limitless echo in the media.  Not to mention, of course, the frequent inanity of a discipline that allows one to conclude that “an economist is an expert who tomorrow will know why something he predicted yesterday did not occur today”[^3], a very unique field in which two individuals can obtain the Nobel Prize after having defended diametrically opposed positions.  Official, academic, and media discourse is often so hilarious that at times, while working on this book, it occurred to me that an anthology of the stories told by the defenders of the existing system might be more fruitful.  It would at least be funnier.

# The Stories

1. ## Salt, rice, and matches

Derzu Uzala is the protagonist of a highly celebrated book in which Vladimir Arseniev narrates the life of a nomadic hunter living in the easternmost lands of Siberia.  The book was successfully adapted into a film by Akira Kurosawa.

Arseniev writes that one day, after waking up from a nap, he found that Dersu had collected firewood and tree bark, and placed them in a cabin.  Arseniev thought that the hunter intended to burn down the cabin, and tried to convince Dersu to not do so.  The hunter, without saying a word, asked for a little salt and a handful of rice while wrapping some matches in bark, along with the salt and the rice.  He hung the two packets on a wall in the hut and prepared to leave.  When Arseniev asked him if he planned to return soon, Derzu shook his head and indicated that someday someone would come to the cabin and would be grateful to find dry wood, matches, and a little food.  Derzu was thinking of a stranger he would never see, who, in turn, would never know whom to thank for the fire and the food.

This story reminds me of another, heard so many times, of how people in the Mediterranean – and many other places – had, and perhaps today still have, the custom of planting slowly-maturing trees.  Knowing very well that they would never benefit from those trees, and evidently thinking of future generations. In the eyes of those of us who received a Western education, it’s hard to understand that trees have spirits.  And yet, as Norbert S. Hill has emphasized, for many indigenous communities, the universe is alive.  When we see members of these communities talking to trees, we’re not witnessing any sort of mental disorder.

According to a saying of the indigenous communities of North America, each generation must secure the future of the next seven.  How far has our ignominy gone, that, in the most enlightened version of ourselves, we merely ask that each generation pass on to the next the same natural capital it inherited from the previous.  In the most enlightened version, I repeat.

2. ## Women’s huts

The name of John Zerzan is associated with anarcho-primitivism.  Zerzan is a fierce critic of technology.  He has frequently categorically stated that all technologies created by capitalism bear the imprint of the division of labor, hierarchy, and exploitation.  It’s a serious argument that merits consideration.

In one of his books, *Twilight of the Machines*, Zerzan revisits the views of Camille Paglia, a well-known literary scholar whose discourse, though feminist in one of its dimensions, arouses many controversies among feminists themselves.  Paglia, perhaps provocatively, recounts once encountering a gigantic crane rising from the bed of a truck on the street.  This sight led her to wonder if this technological prodigy, connecting us back to ancient Egypt, would have been conceivable in a society and history led by women and not, like ours, by men.  Paglia concludes, with a clear vocation to provoke, that with women in charge, we would still be living in straw huts.

The immediate and logical reaction to such a conclusion is to deny the premise, i.e. to assert instead that women would have achieved the same or even greater technological feats than men.  While this is a perfectly respectable response, it avoids the main question: are those modest and despised straw \[*paglia* in Italian\] huts not the most significant and wonderful contribution of women to the development of humanity, far from the miseries of technologies that have so often shown their immense capacity to end life?  In one of her texts Maria González Reyes asks herself, very much in tune with what I am saying, what history would be like if women had written it.  Here is her answer: “It would be history filled with stories of what happens inside kitchens, in neighbor’s patios, of those who cling to brooms and of water that washes away the dirt from the floor.  Stories hanging in the wind, on community clotheslines, held by clothespins on ropes, about to fly off as the wind wishes”.

3. ##  Multiply the objects

Forgive me for staying with the straw huts.  And I do so to retrieve one of the texts included in that remarkable book, *Der Papalagi* (The Papalagi)[^4].  According to this text, if a white population – the aforementioned Papalagi – were forced to live in the characteristic huts of Samoa, they would only suffer and complain, and promptly head into the forest to collect wood, turtle shells, glass, wire, colorful stones, and many other objects and raw materials.  They would work day and night to ensure that their cabins were full of things.  The latter, large and small, would break easily and be at the mercy of fire and rain, so they would have to be constantly replaced.  The wisdom of the Papalagi concludes that the more objects you need, the better European you become.

I have read that, on average, those of us living in wealthy countries possess around ten thousand objects. In contrast, many people in indigenous communities in Latin America have merely a hundred or so items per person. However, it's a given that they are not a hundred times less happy than us. On the contrary, I suspect that their happiness is often strikingly greater, much greater, than ours.

4. ## Work to live, not trade

In an acclaimed book, *The Innocent Anthropologist*, Nigel Barley recounts an experience he had among the inhabitants of the Dowayo community in Cameroon.  These people knew well that the millet they cultivated fetched high prices in the cities of the nation.  However, the prospect of selling their millet in the cities didn’t appeal to them, because the markets were controlled by Fulani merchants who, says Barley, aimed for profits of one to two hundred percent in their transactions.  Since these merchants were also responsible for millet transportation, it was clear to the Dowayo that the payment they would receive for their crops would be minimal at best. Consequently, rather than participate in a system rife with greed and exploitation, the Dowayo community preferred to grow just enough to meet their needs, turning their backs on the seemingly lucrative but exploitative urban market.  

This choice obviously had a beneficial consequence: one could work many fewer hours than otherwise required to satisfy the urban demand for millet. The same was true of much of the European peasantry, in the Middle Ages.  Like the Dowayo, they too were not driven by the urge to accumulate for sale but were satisfied with fulfilling their basic needs, or just a bit more.

But the case of the Dowayo community also brings to mind the notably low productivity that was characteristic, from at least the 1950s, in Soviet-style systems. This lack of productivity was seemingly a form of working-class resistance against the authoritarianism and dysfunction inherent in those systems.  The choice to work more or less should be a free one – something that was not the case in those systems and is not in current capitalist societies.  How many among us wouldn’t prefer to significantly reduce the working day, even at the cost of earning less?  But the rules of the game prevent it, or at least make it difficult.

5. ## Market among the ruins

Years ago, I had an experience in which, in just a few minutes, I came face to face with the paradoxes, the unreasonableness, and the misery of our societies.  I had participated in a debate in a village in eastern Aragon, in Spain, and on a Sunday morning I was taken to Zaragoza to catch a train.  With time to spare, my companion suggested we take a walk around the buildings left over from a water-themed exposition that had taken place in the Aragonese capital.  Everywhere there was evidence of what seemed like a shipwreck.  The large sums invested had left no trace other than abandoned buildings and widespread waste.  Not only that: in the area of the former exhibition there wasn’t a soul in sight.

On the way to the train station I discovered that nearby, very close to the ruins I’ve described, was a flea market, a traditional market.  The vendors had erected their stalls by hand, using iron rods.  There were no grand investments and no designer buildings.  Instead, it was the usual, humble, yet remarkably effective system.  Because the market was teeming with people – in contrast with the deserted buildings that had enriched a few and inflated the egos of a few political leaders.  Life was clearly elsewhere.  Close, but elsewhere.

6. ## A conflictive re-ruralization

When I speak of degrowth or of future collapse in regions that are traditionally described as depressed, I usually draw attention to a paradox: these very regions are likely to fare better in such a collapse. This is largely because, in their current state, they are the least reliant on energies and technologies that are complex and challenging to access.

Collapse will hit harder in cities than in the countryside.  True, not all cities will be affected to the same extent: those with large agricultural areas around them, access to abundant water, located at crossroads, and possessing an ancient and traditional character, will presumably fare better. An example of the opposite model is Detroit, the once-thriving mecca of the American automotive industry, which has already endured a harsh collapse.

Regarding the countryside, it must be acknowledged that, despite what has been said, the scenario will be far from idyllic. The countryside will undoubtedly feel the consequences of our recent actions: the misuse of land, reliance on monoculture, heavy mechanization, and the general commodification of all relationships 

The challenges facing rural areas in the context of collapse extend further. A few years ago, I had the chance to discuss degrowth in Binéfar, a village in Aragon near the Catalonian border. While not a particularly depressed region, Binéfar exemplifies the broader thesis I've outlined, with a significant caveat. I likely lauded Binéfar for its active agricultural scene, plentiful water, and low population density. So, what was the issue for this town and its surrounding region?  The problem lay \- lies \- in the fact that it lies two hundred kilometers from Barcelona, so that in the event of a general collapse, there's a high likelihood of a mass exodus from larger cities, and even smaller ones, as people seek more manageable living conditions. Should this scenario unfold, the initial benefits enjoyed by a depressed region could vanish, as if by magic.

7. ## The price of a stay

Thanks to Fernando Esteve, I have come across a story told by Bruce Chatwin in his book *In Patagonia*. A customer spent the night in a hotel in Río Pico (Argentina) run "by a Jewish family that had no basic notion of what profit was".  As the customer prepared to pay the next morning, this dialogue took place: "'How much do I owe you for the room?'. 'Nothing. If you hadn't slept in it, no one would have.' 'And how much do I owe you for dinner?'. 'Nothing. How could we have known you were coming? We cooked for ourselves.' 'So, how much do I owe you for the wine?'. 'Nothing. We always serve wine to guests.' 'And what about the *mate*?'. 'Nobody pays for *mate.*' 'What can I pay for, then? Only bread and coffee are left.' 'I can't charge you for the bread, but coffee with milk is a *gringo* thing and I'll make you pay for it.'"

Someone will say that a business, let alone an economy in general, cannot function according to practices like those that Chatwin ascribes to this very singular Jewish family – we do well, of course, to break xenophobic prejudices in this regard. And yet, there’s strong evidence that from time immemorial many human societies have conformed to these practices. What's more, they’ve often justified them with the noble principle of reciprocity. This leads me to wonder: how much could we benefit if we embraced a genuine de-marketization of our interactions and relationships? Like the hotel owners, who clearly gave importance to realities other than money.

8. ##  Uncle Arsenio’s milk

The story of Uncle Arsenio shows how the commodification of all relationships unhappily destroys well-functioning systems that generated well-being. Uncle Arsenio owned ten cows that gave him twenty liters of high-quality milk every day. He sold the milk to a local dairy company that paid him fairly and was highly regarded for the quality of its products. Uncle Arsenio lived well, and his business generated income for the veterinarian, his suppliers, the store, the bakery, and the bar.

At a certain point, the dairy company asked Uncle Arsenio to increase milk production. This didn't sit well with him, as he had to take out a loan to buy ten new cows and hire an extra hand. Soon after, the local dairy owners received an offer they couldn’t refuse from a multinational corporation. The new firm soon laid off a large part of the workforce and told Uncle Arsenio he had to lower the price of his milk. These decisions were made far away, and at stake were the dividends that had to be paid to the shareholders. 

Uncle Arsenio had managed to hold on, but now things became difficult. To pay off the loan, he had to let go of his helper and was left alone with the cows at a time when the multinational was demanding an increase in production. As there was no money to buy new cows, the company suggested that Uncle Arsenio buy feed to fatten the animals, even if it affected the quality of the milk. However, with the purchase of the feed, expenses skyrocketed, and our hero found himself in a very delicate situation: he couldn't pay off the loan, he couldn't pay his suppliers, and he wasn't even in a position to live decently himself. He applied for a new loan, which of course was denied, and soon after the bank seized the farm and slaughtered the cows (which surely suffered more than Uncle Arsenio himself).

Today, Uncle Arsenio generates no wealth at all. He receives a monthly aid of 420 euros. He can barely afford a room, electricity, and water, and he frequents the soup kitchen. Meanwhile, the multinational – how surprising\! – closed the milk factory, or rather, moved it to another country where production costs were much lower. We all – they say – came out ahead.

9. ## A Laotian village

International financial organizations are irritated by life. The relationship between the two is vividly illustrated by a story set in Laos, recounted by Pierre Rabhi in one of his books.

A villager recounts life in a Laotian village, home to about two hundred people, located next to a forest and on the banks of a river. The rice they cultivated was their main food. The houses were built with native materials, and the lands were suited to the capabilities of the community’s buffaloes. The harvest was stored in granaries located along the road bordering the village. The cereals, fruits, and legumes that were consumed were supplemented by the fish provided by the river. Rabhi recalls that the logic of mutual support and reciprocity was dominant, exemplified by practices of collective fishing, which allowed for the accumulation of dried fish.

The community took care of widows, the elderly, those without relatives, and, in general, people facing difficulties. A bonze ensured social harmony and acted as a judge. The general viewpoint, inspired by Buddhism, viewed everything as sacred. Only one negative element stood out:  the habitual clearing of land to expand cultivation areas, which was full-fledged aggression against the natural environment. And one wonders about their treatment of animals.

One fine day, an expert from the World Bank visited the village to study its way of life. After his stay, he wrote a report back to the World Bank, remarking that the Laotian community, although deserving of sympathy, couldn’t develop or grow because it spent too much time on unproductive activities. Rabhi's conclusion is straightforward: although the community clearly met the needs of its members, it didn’t generate the type of wealth that the powerful want . It refused to exchange real wealth, the genuine kind, for the dollars of the dominant pseudo-economy.

10. ## The parable of the Mexican fisherman

I tell this story often, and it’s omnipresent in books on degrowth.  In my version, to which I have dedicated a whole book, it’s set in a town of the Mexican coast.

A villager lies half-asleep by the sea. An American tourist approaches and strikes up a conversation with him.  At a certain point the foreigner asks: 'And you, what do you do for a living? What's your job?'. 'I'm a fisherman', replies the Mexican. 'Wow, a very hard job', replies the tourist, who adds: 'I suppose you work many hours every day, right?'. 'Quite a few, yes', responds his interlocutor. 'How many hours do you work on average each day?'. 'I dedicate a couple or three hours to fishing', replies the questioned. 'Two hours? And what do you do with the rest of your time?'. 'I wake up late, fish for a couple of hours, play with my children for a while, take a nap with my wife, and in the evening, I go out to drink a few beers'. 'But, how can you be like this?', the American tourist reacts angrily. 'What do you mean? I don't understand your question'. 'Why don't you work more? If you did, in a couple of years you’d have a bigger boat'. 'And for what?'. 'Later on, you could set up a factory here in the village'. 'And for what?'. 'Over time, you’d set up an office in the capital'. 'And for what?'. 'Years later you’d open branches in the United States and Europe'. 'And for what?'. 'Finally, your company's shares would be listed on the stock exchange and you’d be immensely rich'. 'And all that, for what?', inquires the Mexican. 'Well' \- replies the tourist \-, 'when you’re, I don't know, 65 or 70 years old, you can retire peacefully and come to live here in this village, to wake up late, fish for a couple of hours, play with your grandchildren, take a nap with your wife, and go out in the evening to drink a few beers'.

I want to highlight two dimensions to this tale. Firstly, the Mexican protagonist isn't a revolutionary, nor does he advocate for an alternate social order. His request is simple: to be left alone. This desire comes from a clear disdain for the chaos and misery of capitalist frenzy. Second, the story contains many omissions. Suffice it to say that it fails to tell us how many hours the Mexican protagonist's wife worked...

11. ##  Objections to the parable of the fisherman.

The story of the Mexican fisherman is clearly a parable; its content should not be interpreted literally.  I think of it as a clear tribute to the condition  of the fisherman, compared to that of the annoying and commercialized American tourist.  Occasionally, however, I have encountered people who are very critical of this view.

A few years ago, during a public event, an elderly man spoke up to defend what he described as a life, his own, of strenuous work to support a very humble family. I tried to explain what parables are, assured him that I understood his viewpoint perfectly, and ventured to ask if, despite everything, he had ever seen the Mexican fisherman in himself.  And of course I added that the very respectable character of my interlocutor’s way of life would disappear if it involved the exploitation of other human beings.

The second controversy I remember was more substantial. It took place on one of the social networks. A Mexican, who as far as I could tell was an honest anti-colonialist, reacted angrily after watching a video of me telling the story. In his view, I was lamentably ignorant of the grim condition of Mexican fishermen, and in any case, he said, the story was meaningless. Once again I found myself explaining parables, and that the thrust of the story is its defense of the fisherman against the colonial and productivist intrusion of the tourist. Still indignant, he asked me why I chose a Mexican fisherman instead of a Spanish one. I had to answer that unfortunately most Spanish fishermen think the same as the American tourist.  Although I suspect he eventually grasped the parable's true meaning and realized his initial misunderstanding, he chose to block me and leave. How many people in Mexico, fully conscious of the impacts of colonization like this man, might have smiled at the parable, finding a sense of identification with the fisherman.

12. ## How we die

I often come across texts that call attention to something important, very important, which we nevertheless overlook. In the rich North, over the past few decades, the manner of dying has changed. Specifically, I'm referring to deaths that don't occur abruptly or unexpectedly, but rather as the culmination of an inevitable process, driven either by illness or advanced age.

In times past, it was common to die at home. This was certainly true in rural areas, but also in cities. People usually died surrounded by the attention and affection of their loved ones. The technological paraphernalia to which we are accustomed today didn’t exist. Nor did the sometimes harmful effects of hospitals and their regulations. This older way of dying served to alleviate both physical pain and pain linked to death itself and its rosary of grief and loneliness.  It’s hardly surprising that even today, many elderly people prefer to die at home. They have their reasons.

It’s well known that today, what I have described is uncommon. We die in an unfamiliar environment, a hospital, often in a closed and monitored enclosure far removed from our loved ones, surrounded by devices as strange as they are annoying, and often subject to a myriad of strict legal rules. Paradoxically, this environment doesn't always alleviate physical pain, even as it seems to increase the pain of loss.

I recognize that in comparing the old and the new, there’s an important fact: nowadays we live many more years than did our ancestors who died in their homes. However, even this seemingly clear advantage is not without controversy. Some believe we are pushing against our biological limits, with not inconsiderable negative consequences. This raises a fundamental question: Are we actually happy during these additional years we've gained?

13. ## Rafa Nadal’s private jet

It seems that in the perception of many, the two Spaniards whose honor and dignity best reflect the virtues of their country are businessman Amancio Ortega and sportsman Rafael Nadal.  It’s not my place to comment on the former’s business skills and the latter’s racket skills; I want to examine another aspect of the issue.

Amancio Ortega is known for frequently making significant donations to the Spanish public health system, for purchasing specialized equipment. “How nice”, someone might say, with a touch of naivety. These donations are however clearly intended to mask the fact that Ortega faces a myriad of difficulties, because of his fiscal irregularities. And probing deeper, we see that these problems are minor compared to the exploitation of so many human beings \- including, of course, minors \- that has characterized the behavior of Zara’s audacious owner. Many choose to focus solely on the spectacle of the donations, overlooking what lies behind. With the brazen support of most of the miscommunication media.

As for Rafael Nadal, I have an anecdote that graphically illustrates some people’s curious preferences. A few weeks ago, a Madrid newspaper, *ABC,* reported that Nadal often travels on his own private plane. No surprises so far. What was interesting was that, according to the newspaper, there are no luxuries of any kind on Nadal's private jet. (I suppose that, in an exercise of self-restraint, the journalist preferred to not say that Nadal’s plane also has zero environmental impact \- why make a spectacle of it?) As someone has suggested on social networks, the tennis player likely drinks his Moët Chandon out of disposable paper cups. Because, after all, who among us hasn't enjoyed the luxury of a private jet conceived for the humble?

14. ## The intricacies of business communication

We don’t pay enough attention to business communication – it’s a domain full of riches. A few years ago I boarded a flight in Madrid, on my way to Galicia. The plane left with a three-hour delay, during which we were forced to sit, immobile, in the cabin. At a certain point, the flight attendant, who clearly had a sense of humor, addressed the passengers. He reported that on the previous flight, to explain a delay, they had suggested that the plane had been abducted by aliens, adding that sadly it wasn’t true.  Because, he added, categorically, the explanation for the delay was different: the company overexploited the planes and did the same to its employees.

The flight attendant's words fell like a bombshell among the passengers. In half a minute he managed to defuse what could’ve become a revolt. Knowing that the airline’s employees were in a worse situation than ours, it was only logical to keep quiet. My takeaway was that we had just witnessed a complex and somewhat risky example of corporate communication, that even at the risk of tarnishing the airline's image, effectively defused a tense situation.  Of course, other interpretations were possible, including the idea that the flight attendant had been fired that day and was seeking revenge with his announcement. But that seems rather unlikely.

I recall a second instance, which, perhaps, points to the logic of sabotage. Some time ago, I read a newspaper advertisement for a bank. The premise of the advertisement was genuine nonsense. By investing half a million euros to an investment fund, anyone willing to take such a significant risk would be entitled to a trip to London for two to attend a Premier League match. The offer was clearly ridiculous: for someone with half a million euros, a plane ticket, a night in a hotel, and a ticket to a football match would hardly trigger interest in the bank. Even if it was for two people.

But what really drew my eyes to the advertisement was the specific wording of the main phrase, which read: 'For *only* half a million euros...'. The use of the word 'only' in this context only made sense by imagining the figure of someone who, in an advertising studio, chose to express revenge through irony.  Sabotage, in short. I chose to keep my half a million euros in another bank.

15. ##  Saving time, for what?

The contemporary world is full of gadgets that promise to save us time. It isn’t clear, though, what we are to do with the time we have saved. Are we in an endless vortex, in which we spend it looking for even more gadgets to save time?

There is a passage from a famous book by Antoine de Saint-Exupéry, *Le Petit Prince* (The Little Prince), that talks about this.  "'Good morning,' said the little prince. 'Good morning,' replied the merchant. He was a seller of sophisticated pills which appeased thirst. One swallows one a week,  
and one no longer feels the need to drink. “Why do you sell that?” Said the little prince.  “It’s a big saving of time,” said the merchant. Experts have made calculations. We save fifty-three minutes a week.  “And what do we do for fifty-three minutes?”  “We make what we want …,” said the little prince, “if I had fifty-three minutes to spend, I would walk slowly towards a fountain.”

Some people use their time more wisely than the little prince. Take, for instance, the hundreds of millions of human beings who happily spend the time they’ve saved watching television. It’s hardly surprising, then, that we're desperately seeking ways to save more and more time. Fountains and water are of no interest to us.

16. ## We live less and less

On the same topic, our unfortunate use of time, I’d like to add an observation that has a suggestively pedagogical dimension, also because of its graphic character. From the mid-20th century to the present, especially in the affluent North, we've effectively gained an average of three hours per day.  That is, today we live 24 hours for every 21 hours that our grandmothers and grandfathers enjoyed.

And yet, this apparently healthy gain is offset by the more than four hours a day we spend watching television \- in the United States, it's estimated that people spend as much as three entire years of their life watching the idiot box \-.  We dedicate an hour and a half a day commuting to work, tired and with bags under our eyes.  And many of us spend an almost unlimited time navigating social networks.

The conclusion is clear: if we subtract all these hours, which I’ll allow myself to label  as lost time, it turns out that we live, on average, quite a lot less than our grandparents. Perhaps that's why our ancestors, despite the challenging environments they faced, were generally happier than we are today. At least, that's how it seems to me.

17. ##  For austerity

I have always thought that the word “austerity” refers to a healthy trait. When we say of a person that they are austere, we are unequivocally stating a virtue, especially if we contrast that quality with an adjective like “wasteful”. We’re in some sense rediscovering the qualities of people from another era – who, it’s true, may have had no choice but to be austere. To one degree or another, they made a virtue out of necessity.

However, it seems that our rulers, who aren’t always foolish, have stolen the word from us. I first noticed this years ago, when the European confederation of trade unions called for mobilizations against austerity. The situation became even more apparent when I came across the unfortunate and almost incomprehensible term “austericide.” 

The sleight of hand wasn't hard to decipher: government circles were labeling measures that were essentially obscene and indefensible cuts in social spending as 'austerity.' They were cutting exactly what, ironically, should not be cut, justifying these decisions under the guise of austerity – a concept that, in my view, still deserves respect.  Although I suppose that at this point that battle is already lost. Yet another.

18. ## Planned obsolescence

Planned obsolescence is by no means a new phenomenon. A well-known example will suffice. I have in mind a light bulb, conceived by Adolphe Chaillet and manufactured by the Shelby Electric Company in 1895, which is still functioning today. Major electrical companies quickly agreed to end such unprofitable economic nonsense, and began producing light bulbs that stopped working after a short time. They also prevented Narva light bulbs, produced in East Germany and significantly more durable than those manufactured by the giants of the capitalist world, from entering the markets of Western countries.

The phenomenon is apparent everywhere. A long time ago a formula was found that made women's stockings almost unbreakable. Again, bad for business. We know that most printers are programmed to stop working after just a few printed sheets. It’s obvious that decades ago our appliances lasted longer, much longer.

Let’s not forget that planned obsolescence also has other more subtle manifestations. Latouche points to a clearly induced form of behavior that surely benefits manufacturers and distributors: people drive to the supermarket once a week, fill their cart with the latest deals, fill the refrigerator with the purchased items, heat the food in the microwave and, time and again, discover that many of those products have passed their expiration date....

19. ##  Latouche on Africa

Serge Latouche, whom I just mentioned, has dedicated his life to studying and theorizing about degrowth, working extensively in Africa. I once asked him if his theory of degrowth was largely indebted to his personal experience on that continent; he replied immediately that yes, it was.

One of Latouche's books is titled *L'autre Afrique* (The Other Africa). There is a moment in the book that I want to highlight. Latouche writes that in Europe there are people – surely not many, but they exist – who have a guilty conscience in relation to Africa, i.e. are aware of the aftermath of five centuries of the North’s plundering of the human and material wealth of the continent. This guilty conscience is often expressed as the idea that we must help the people of Africa. Latouche provocatively asks: wouldn’t it instead be smarter to let them help us? On numerous occasions, and in conditions of extreme poverty, the inhabitants of Africa have demonstrated their ability to develop networks of solidarity that have effectively solved many problems. In the rich North, on the other hand, we have quite dramatically lost this capacity. Perhaps we are still in time to recover it.

If we want to help Africa \- Latouche continues, provocatively \- the best thing we can do is leave its inhabitants alone. They were much happier five centuries ago, before the arrival of our ancestors. That said, if we have a vaccine against AIDS, we should give it to them. Without, of course, letting business get in the way. 

20. ## Summers and pollution in poor countries

When someone asks me about the philosophy underlying capitalist globalization, I usually mention a frequent remark by Vandana Shiva, the Indian anti-globalization activist, about a memo that Lawrence Summers, chief economist of the World Bank, is said to have written on the subject of environmental economics.

Larry Summers uses three arguments to justify relocating polluting industries to poorer countries. Firstly, since wages are lower in these countries, the costs of pollution, primarily manifested in an increase in diseases and deaths, will also be lower. Secondly, many parts of the Third World have lower levels of environmental degradation, so his logic is to pollute where there has been less pollution before. Summer writes that “I've always thought that under-populated countries in Africa  
are vastly UNDER-polluted, their air quality is probably vastly inefficiently low  
compared to Los Angeles or Mexico City.” The third argument is that since poor people are, well, poor, they are less likely to be concerned about environmental issues. Summers elaborates: “The concern over an agent that causes a one in a million change in the odds of prostate cancer is obviously going to be much higher in a country where people survive to get prostate cancer than in a country where under 5 mortality is 200 per thousand.” 

Such a grim argument led Vandana Shiva to conclude that, apparently, in the eyes of some economic schools, human life has a different value in the wealthy North and the impoverished South. In a dramatic illustration of the way things are going on the planet, Lawrence Summers held a prominent position in the economic team of U.S. President Barack Obama. A word to the wise...

21. ## Rats and Mutual Aid

Óscar Horta recalls an experiment that was conducted decades ago at Brown University, in the United States. In my view it demonstrates that feelings of solidarity in response to the suffering of others is by no means unknown among non-human animals, indicating the omnipresent logic of mutual support.

In the experiment, caged rats had to press a lever in order to obtain food.  They soon internalized the mechanism.  In a subsequent phase of the experiment, when the animals pressed the lever, an electric shock was generated in a nearby cage, and the painful effects were visible on the other rats.  On seeing this, the animals in the first cage stopped pressing the lever, even at the cost of not getting the food.  

Horta adds that there are other experiments suggesting similar conclusions regarding solidarity with suffering animals. One of them showed that when given the choice between obtaining food and freeing fellow rats trapped in a water tank, the rats most often chose the latter. I suspect that Kropotkin would have been extremely interested to know this. His theory of mutual aid would have been significantly strengthened.

22. ## 70% of the poor are women

According to an estimate, 70% of poor people \- and 78% of the illiterate \- on the planet are women. I always insist on this point: these percentages are very telling.  We’re not talking about 52% of women being poor compared to 48% of men. We’re referring to the vast difference between 70 and 30 percent. According to another estimate, which I admit is controversial, women do 67% of the work but receive only 10% of the income.

Large numbers, despite how striking they seem, only go so far.  We need to get down to the nitty-gritty and delve into what they mean. I do so with the help of this text by María González Reyes: "There are women who have never worn a new piece of clothing. There are women who do not have a tap in their homes from which water comes out and yet manage to cook. There are women who have never owned anything. There are women who have never urinated in a bathroom. There are women who have never entered a shopping center. There are women who have never lived in a neighborhood with sewage. There are women who every day wonder if they will get some food. There are women who have never been free to decide what they wanted to do with their bodies. There are women whose daughters and sons die from easily curable diseases. There are women who have never had labor rights. There are women who have never had any rights. There are women who have never thought about the future. There are women for whom the future is not another time, it is another place. There are women who do not live, who survive. There are women who have forgotten to breathe. There are women who live in the other world and who, without gritting their teeth, never forget to take care of the people around them".

I’ll allow myself to draw a quick conclusion: those who think that the age-old problems of symbolic and material marginalization of women are on a fortunate path to resolution are, sadly, turning their backs on reality.

23. ## Who will pay us our wages on Saturday?

When we consider the difficulties we may face in the future, we forget that those difficulties are set to present themselves – or have already presented themselves – because we’ve succeeded in escaping the dead end we were in, and that, ultimately, we’re illuminating a new world.   This brings to mind the richly ironic lyrics of a French anarchist song, presumably from the 19th century.  After calling for the abolition of capital, the song asked who was going to pay us our wages on Saturday once capital is abolished? It’s quite striking, this refusal to accept that the question of wages hardly matters if capital were to end – and in any case has a different meaning from the inherited one.

I’m not sure if what follows from the above is a frank defense of realism in action or, on the contrary, an equally frank rejection of another realism, this one unmistakably demobilizing.  When I hear someone say something is not realistic, all my alarms go off. And I’m reminded of a statement, loud and rude, by Georges Bernanos, who once said that realism is the good conscience of the bastards. They invoke reality as if it were given by nature and literally unchangeable, when in fact, they themselves have shaped the reality to which they refer, which blatantly serves their lowest and most selfish interests. In these circumstances, I suspect we have no choice but to be proudly unrealistic. And to abolish capital. 

24. ## Buy it again

In one of their stories, our friends at *Econoplastas* tell us of a character, Lorenzo, who lived for his car. He washed it twice a week, took it out for a walk every day, as if it were a little dog, scrupulously tried not to strain it, and took it in for check-ups earlier than recommended. Six months after buying the car, the ashtray lid popped open. Although he tried to shut it in every which way, the slightest movement would cause it to reopen. Some spring must have come loose. Annoyed at the prospect of his beloved car having a defect, he decided to take it to the garage.

In the garage, they made matters clear from the start: it wasn’t worth fixing the ashtray, and instead it would be better to buy a new car. Why? The ashtray was embedded in the front of the dashboard and was a very particular piece. To reach the base of the broken spring, the entire dashboard had to be disassembled, which in turn was a single piece containing the light switches, the turn signal, the windshield wiper control, the heating and air conditioning regulators, the ignition key... As Lorenzo listened astonished, the mechanic explained that they also had to consider what would happen with the odometer, the tachometer, the oil pressure, the water temperature, the car's computer, the radio, the CD player, the MP3 player, and the USB port. Things didn’t end there, however, as they had to weigh what would happen with the wiring, the connectors, the anti-parasite devices, the cable ties, and the terminals embedded in the steering column, which would have to be disassembled. 

“If we're lucky, and with great care, we could disassemble the steering column from above, which saves labor, but risks damaging the wiring harness. Otherwise, we’ll have to go in via the engine, affecting the steering box, the Bendix cylinder, the ball bearings, the Carcan joint, the comb arm, and the rack. Plus, we’d have to order the spring from Thailand, plus VAT and labor... As I said, it's not worth it, buy a new one'. Poor Lorenzo. If only he had been more careful with the spring in his ashtray.

25. ## The *Quieto* Protocol

In my family’s case, and I suppose this is true for many others in at least moderately rich countries, each new generation travels more than the previous one. I believe my paternal grandmother never left Galicia, and my maternal grandparents moved little more than between Galicia itself, Madrid, and Valencia. My parents never crossed the Spanish borders, except for a brief post-war trip my mother made to Lisbon and, later, with my father, to Porto. While I have set foot in – I won't say actually know – about fifty countries, my daughter, a quarter of a century old, threatens to soon surpass that record. Unless, of course, collapse gets in the way.

Years ago, when there was much discussion about the Kyoto Protocol, an appealing idea gained traction in the environmental community: the notion of adopting the *Quieto* \[stay still\] Protocol instead. Our commitment to the planet called for traveling much less, and enjoying the many attractions of our own local area, or those within reasonable proximity. It’s important to remember that while in a not-so-distant year like 1970, our single planet could reasonably meet our needs, things have since changed for the worse. By 1980, we had used up our annual resource allocation by November 3, by 1990 it was October 11, by 2000 September 23, by 2010 August 8, and by 2019 July 29\. To sustain the lifestyle that humanity – or more precisely, its privileged segments – has adopted, we now need 1.7 planets. And it seems we don’t have them.

26. ## Thinking about collapse

When I speak about the impending collapse, I often employ a pair of vignettes that illustrate aspects of our attitude towards it. The first is about someone at a port who needs to travel by boat to an island. A friend informs them that there's a 7% risk of the boat sinking during the journey. It's obvious that no one in their right mind would board such a boat, unless they had an absolutely urgent need to reach the island immediately. Nevertheless, though I firmly believe that the risk of a general collapse of our system is well above 7% percent, every day we still get on that boat.

The second vignette asks us to imagine that tomorrow, a prestigious research center predicts that without doubt in 2072 a gigantic asteroid will collide with planet Earth. Logically, the day after tomorrow the system of the United Nations, the governments of different states, the armed forces, research centers, and universities would begin working to determine what can be done to avoid this impact. Although the conditions that are beginning to crush us in the form of climate change and the exhaustion of energy raw materials, are increasingly comparable to the mass of the asteroid, we prefer to look the other way. Perhaps this is because the impending collapse is the product of human action through a system called *capitalism*. And not a natural disaster.

27. ## Diogenes’ lentils

Anthony de Mello recounts in one of his texts that one day the philosopher Diogenes was eating lentils for dinner.  Another philosopher, Aristippus, who lived well because he consistently flattered the king time and again, said to Diogenes: 'If you would learn to submit to the king, you would not have to eat those miserable lentils, Diogenes'. The latter replied very quickly: 'If you, Aristippus, had learned, as you should, to eat lentils, you would not have to flatter the king'.

This story reminds me of how many bonds we end up accepting in order to access goods and privileges that in fact contribute nothing substantial to our lives. We would gain much if we could distance ourselves from the voluntary servitude discussed by La Boétie and, likewise, from such dependencies. A few weeks ago, during a public event, an elderly lady said something that seemed very sensible to me. She argued that personal austerity, refraining from unnecessary spending, the intention to work less and in a field we enjoy, the desire to enhance our personal relationships, and the resolve to rebel, should all be our guides regardless of what happens around us – whether the ecological crisis worsens or not, whether collapse is imminent or not, and whether international summits serve any purpose or not.

I often come across people who, even if in good faith, believe that those of us advocating  for practices of degrowth would be incapable of maintaining a complex society like the present one, and of satisfying all our needs.  Like Diogenes, I can only respond that our goal isn’t at all to maintain such a complex society, and that we reserve the right to question the value of these so-called needs. Lentils, well cooked, are very tasty. Diogenes knew this perfectly well.

28. ## Collapse now and avoid the rush

I am often asked if we’ll be able to avoid the collapse. I usually answer that, in my intuition \- I emphasize that it’s just that, an intuition \- the answer is no. What I believe is within our capability is to mitigate some of the more negative effects of this collapse and, possibly, delay its full manifestation for a little while.

I must confess, however, that I don’t know if postponing the collapse by a little is in our interest.  This reminds me of a book by John Michael Greer, whose title in English is somewhat humorously *Collapse Now and Avoid the Rush*.  The core argument is that if collapse is inevitable, wouldn't it be better to start adopting the characteristics of a post-collapse society now, while the most dramatic consequences of climate change haven’t fully taken hold and significant reserves of energy resources still exist?  Wouldn't it be smarter, in other words, to start building an alternative society based on degrowth, re-ruralization, de-technologization, de-patriarchalization, de-colonization, de-complexification, and the de-marketization of all relationships? Even if the operation did not fully succeed, we would have learned something, no? And – allow me the irony, which I owe to Antonio Turiel – if things go wrong, we could quietly return to the miseries of a society that is on the brink of collapse...

29. ## Transportation in the United States and in China

Many years ago, I came across an article written by a Nobel Prize-winning economist, which I've since lost track of. In this piece, Wassily Leontieff made a comparison between the transportation systems of the United States and China. It's important to note that, contrary to appearances, the author's intention was not to evaluate two macro-economic systems, e.g. liberal capitalism versus whatever system existed in China at the time. We could substitute the name of China with Thailand or Myanmar, and I suspect the argument would remain largely unchanged.

The Nobel laureate began by pointing out that the United States has the most developed transportation system in the world. The country had a vast network of highways and freeways, the highest number of cars per inhabitant, and the highest levels of gasoline consumption on the planet. However, when it came to assessing how this hyper-developed transportation system met daily needs, its problems became apparent. The average inhabitant of the United States lived 55 minutes by car from their workplace, had to get up very early each morning, often found themselves engulfed in gigantic traffic jams that were damaging to their nerves and to the environment, only to frequently end up arriving late to work.

At that time – I am speaking, it should be remembered, of several decades ago – China barely made an appearance in international statistical yearbooks. There were neither highways nor freeways in the country, the number of privately owned cars was very low, and the per capita consumption of gasoline was laughable. And yet, the average inhabitant lived five minutes by bicycle from their workplace, could sleep an hour more each morning than their American counterpart, and never found themselves caught in formidable traffic jams that damaged their nerves (and the environment) – and usually arrived on time to work.

Leontieff wondered, of course, which of these two transportation systems was objectively more developed.  He made it clear that he didn't want to overlook the fact that most of the Chinese population probably didn't consume enough calories to lead a dignified life. That must be said.

30. ## The metaphor of the water lily

The metaphor of the water lily is well known. It serves to illustrate how easy it is to be wrong when assessing the damage we have caused to the earth’s environment. And to draw attention to the speed with which processes that escape our control make their effects felt.

Imagine a lake in which there is a water lily. We know that lilies double in number every day: if there is one water lily on Monday, there will be two on Tuesday. Let’s say we also know that the lake will be completely covered with water lilies after twenty days, at which point the plants, lacking vital space, will die off. If I asked you on which day the lake is half-covered in lilies,  you might quickly and spontaneously reason that the answer is the tenth. However, as you’ll soon realize, that’s incorrect. The correct answer is the nineteenth day: since lilies double daily, if they cover half the lake on the nineteenth day, they’ll completely fill it on the twentieth.

On the eighteenth day, the water lilies will cover a quarter of the lake, on the seventeenth an eighth, and on the sixteenth a sixteenth. Let's say we are on the sixteenth. Someone will say: it's not so bad what we've done, since we've only damaged a sixteenth of the planet's surface. However, someone wiser will counter: we’ve set in motion an incredibly rapid process, so that we only have four days left. And the more we postpone taking action, the more the effort, resources, and energy will be required.

31. ## The farce of the automobile, and of the ecological car.

There are two texts by Eduardo Galeano that confront us with the grim reality of the automobile and its world. The first, appearing in *Espejos* \[Mirrors\], sees the car as the spoiled child of the family, sadly gluttonous \- devouring oil, gas, corn, and whatever else is available \-, master of the time of humans who supposedly enjoy it, caught in a whirlwind of stubborn reproduction, a killer on streets and highways, intent on stealing our air, and sarcastic when it hears us say 'I drive'.

The second is found in another book, *El cazador de historias* (The Hunter of Stories), and describes a religious ceremony that took place in Quito, Ecuador, in mid-1972, amidst formidable media attention. After singing the national anthem and with emotions running high, the first barrel of oil produced in the country by Texaco was displayed on a specially constructed altar, adorned with flowers. Galeano narrates how, in a moment of popular fervor, General Rodríguez Lara, who had handed over the business to the American company, shouted: 'We are going to sow oil\! A new era has been born\!'. That era, of course, was marked by the most ferocious massacres of indigenous people in the history of the Amazon rainforest.

I want to add a final observation on the car and its world, drawing attention to the contemporary farce that is the *ecological car*. I will set aside the fact that production of this type of car is as environmentally costly as the conventional vehicle, and that these cars pollute less through the exhaust pipe, but more because of the myriad of gadgets inside them. What I want to emphasize – in line with an idea I explore elsewhere in this little book – is that those who run the planet often manage to prevent us from asking the important questions. In this case, the most relevant question we should ask is whether we need cars at all. The automotive industry has already decided for us that we do, and offers them to us wrapped in a supposed ecological commitment – which fails to mask all the misery behind it.

32. ## Virtues of high speed

Among contemporary Spanish myths, that of the AVE, of high speed railways, stands out with a force of its own.  It’s materialized in trains for the rich, that, unstainable, consume vast amounts of energy, and paradoxically lead to the striking phenomenon of railway desertification.

A few years ago, the then Spanish president, José Luis Rodríguez Zapatero, inaugurated a high-speed section between Córdoba and Antequera, in Andalusia. The selling point of that inauguration was the idea that the train journey between Granada and Madrid was reduced from six hours to four and a half. A couple of days later, a letter from a Granada resident appeared in a newspaper reporting that he had conducted the experiment and certified that it was true: he had saved an hour and a half of his time on the trip to Madrid, and the same returning to Granada. Each of those saved hours, however, cost him an additional 18 euros. The writer admitted to preferring the old train, which allowed him to read an hour and a half more per trip and which, above all \- and let's not fool ourselves \- saved him money he couldn’t spare.

A few years later, in 2010, the then Spanish Minister of Development, José Blanco, called a press conference in Madrid to promote a new high-speed line that was to connect Madrid and Valencia. In his speech, he presented a disheartening estimate. The new line was to allow a 55% reduction in air traffic between these two cities, a 25% reduction in car traffic, and a 5% reduction in bus traffic. I retranslate the inescapable meaning of these numbers: we have invested millions of euros to build a new public railway infrastructure, designed to allow half of the wealthy individuals who traveled by plane between Madrid and Valencia to switch to the train, while the overwhelming majority of the poor who used the bus will continue doing so.  This doesn't seem like the best use of everyone's resources.

A colleague from Andalusia stated at the time that the AVE is a textbook example of how members of the working classes joyfully celebrate the fact their taxes pay for high-speed lines built almost exclusively for the wealthy. And if someone asks who benefits from the AVE, the answer must be that, apart from construction companies and those involved in *la mordida* \[kickbacks\], its main beneficiaries are – what a paradox – private bus companies.

33. ## Felipe González, *el olvidadizo* \[the forgetful one\].

Felipe González, the former Spanish prime minister, was once asked how we’re to address the challenges arising from the ongoing exhaustion of energy raw materials. Three major measures should be taken, he answered: deploying clean and renewable energies, diversifying supply sources, and \- I believe I quote literally \- cautiously reopening the debate on nuclear energy, or, which is the same in González's newspeak, building new nuclear power plants.

Rather than commenting on what González said, I’m interested in drawing attention to what, significantly, he didn’t say. In any scenario of energy scarcity, the first and most basic response must be to call for reducing energy consumption.  Why didn’t González take this first step? It’s possible he was having a bad day – it can happen to anyone – but I believe the main explanation lies elsewhere: Felipe González had been receiving substantial payments from a powerful power company for many years. His silence was a guilty silence: he was simply defending the interests of the company that paid him.

To confront the challenging energy scenario looming before us, we must adopt two major measures simultaneously. First, as González said, we must indeed deploy clean and renewable energy sources, but not to sustain our wasteful and extravagant lifestyle.  Because, quite prosaically, these energy sources are insufficient for that lifestyle. The second measure requires, inexorably, a reduction in energy consumption – something we can easily achieve. Even if it’s not in the interests of the company that paid González. 

34. ## Seminars and Mutual Aid

The implementation of the so-called Bologna Process in European universities brought about significant changes in pedagogy and in the allocation of time to various activities: it increased the emphasis on seminars, a feature that certainly existed before, but had previously held lesser importance. When I had to adapt to this change, I decided that the best way to approach the task was to organize debates with the students.

In the heat of these debates, during the first year of the plan’s implementation, I was unpleasantly surprised that the majority of the participants – there were, of course, exceptions – resonated with the idea that societies progress only through the most ferocious competition and ruthless elbowing.  Challenging this perception is no easy task, primarily because it does, undeniably, reflect a significant part of reality. It would be absurd to deny that there is a competitive dimension to human nature. All I could do was emphasize that, fortunately, this complex nature also includes powerful elements of solidarity and cooperation.

On occasion, I ventured to suggest reading a book that I consider wonderful: *Mutual Aid*, by Kropotkin, one of the most famous anarchist thinkers at the turn of the 19th and 20th centuries. Kropotkin was also an internationally renowned scientist. In his book he empirically demonstrates that numerous species of animals thrive through solidarity and cooperation. It’s crucial to emphasize the biological dimension of the argument, which explains \- in my understanding- why, despite the existence of capital, States, and patriarchies, the practice of solidarity has not died.

35. ## Sobriety and generations

While I'm certain that many of our concepts have a generational dimension, we seldom pay attention to this aspect. It seems clear to me that terms like "voluntary simplicity" and "sobriety" hold different meanings for someone born in 1940, someone born in 1970, and for a twenty-year-old today.

Let me share two experiences that support this conclusion. The first relates to a figure that has nearly vanished from Spanish trains: an elderly man or woman who begins to eat in the carriage, taking out a sandwich and a piece of fruit. The reverence with which they handled these foods could only be explained by a scarcity they must have experienced in their life. Those of us born later have a cooler, and I believe, less intelligent relationship with food: we take for granted that it's always there, readily available to us. This is a serious mistake, especially considering what might be looming on the horizon.

Secondly, every year until recently, I would receive a phone call in Madrid from a group of elderly women, inquiring about my availability to give a talk. About a decade ago, they asked what I wanted to discuss, and I chose to talk about degrowth. On the day of my speech, however, I didn't have time to prepare, which usually meant taking a moment to consider the audience and, if necessary, reorienting arguments, examples, or anecdotes. So, I spoke to these ladies about degrowth as I usually do, but I did so fearing they might not understand me, potentially leading to a conflictual situation.

That wasn't the case. I think they quickly grasped what the degrowth perspective entails. Perhaps they looked back on a difficult time in their youth – the darkest and most obscurantist days of Franco's regime –, remembered what sobriety and voluntary simplicity meant back then, and concluded that these values were indeed worthwhile. I'm thinking of a time when a sweater had to last a decade and throwing away a crust of bread was unthinkable. These good women retained in their minds a pristine space that, when appropriately stimulated, could yield pleasant surprises.

36. ## The business of security

It's interesting to delve into life's paradoxes, and in particular  into the paradoxes of capitalist life. A colleague told me that homes with security alarms are broken into more often than those without. Why? The reason is simple: installing an alarm signals there’s something valuable inside worth protecting – and presumably worth stealing. Additionally, I've been told that the police often take a long time to respond to these alarms. In such circumstances, it’s not surprising that the idea has crossed my mind to start a company that sells expensive signs saying, "We’re sorry: no security alarm is operating in this home."

When I posted about this on social media, I received several stories that, to varying degrees, relate to these paradoxes. I recall a friend telling me about someone who installed a reinforced door in an apartment they were buying with their hard-earned money. The day they were evicted for being unable to pay the mortgage, they decided to rip out and take the door with them. My father's experience was less traumatic. Long ago, he also installed a reinforced door in his house. I felt compelled to point out that the door was by far the most valuable item in his otherwise modest home. Let’s see if they steal it …

37. ## The Great Reckoning

A couple of times in this book, I've mentioned the surprising persistence of practices of solidarity, under conditions that for a long time now have been marked by capital, by the State, and by patriarchal society. Jorge Riechmann, in one of his texts, revisits the opinions of Ken Booth, who contemplates a “Great Reckoning” with deeply entrenched and regressive ways of thinking and behaving. In this judgment, history will reckon with these forms, as conceived by Booth:

“4000 years of patriarchy (the idea that men know best and should dominate society); 2000 years of proselytizing religions (the belief that ‘our’ faith – whichever it is \- represents the only ‘True’ way and so deserves to be universalised); 500 years of capitalism (a fantastically successful means of production, but one that requires losers as well as winners – with nature being amongst the most prominent losers); 350 years of statism/nationalism (the game of sovereignty allied with national narcissism, producing the conflictual logic of international politics, characterized by the pursuit above all of the ‘national interest’ in the context of habitual mistrust and the institution of war); 200 years or so of racism (the idea of superior and inferior human beings, based on minor biological differences); and 100 years or so of consumer democracy (which has led to what J.K. Galbraith called a ‘culture of contentment’ on the part of the winners within and between societies, whereas the losers inhabit lives of exploitation. 

The interaction through history of this combination of ideas and their related  
power-structures has constructed a world that doesn’t work for countless  
millions of its inhabitants, doesn’t work for nature, and doesn’t promise a  
harmonious future.”

Riechmann comments that even though *Homo sapiens sapiens* has been on the planet for about 200,000 years, it has taken just a century and a half of industrial society to bring us to the brink of an abyss. We must ask ourselves – and I add this – whether under these circumstances, we are compelled to reject everything that the ideology of progress has described and, more importantly, promised us.

38. ## Peasantry

A few years ago, I had the opportunity to speak about degrowth at three secondary schools in rural Galicia. It hadn’t occurred to me before, but I asked the hundred or so teenagers in each auditorium if anyone had some awareness, however basic, of the labor involved in farming. Only three hands went up, one in each group. If this was the case in rural Galicia – I thought – it seemed pointless to ask a similar question in cities like Madrid or Barcelona. We city dwellers know nothing about farmers’ chores.

A few months later, I took part in a roundtable discussion on food sovereignty in Albacete, La Mancha. I listened intently to a man my age who began by describing himself as most definitely a *peasant*.  And he clearly was just that, not an *agricultor* \[agriculturist\] – the latter term being firmly rooted in the 'Newspeak' of the European Union and its so-called common agricultural policy. He explained that the few young people remaining in his village, living off their parents, weren’t in the least interested in farming potatoes, tomatoes, and peppers. The shame they seemed to feel for their ancestors' work lit a bulb in my head: perhaps among those young people in Galicia who listened to me – I'm not sure how attentively – many more were familiar with fieldwork than those who had raised their hands.  Perhaps the former were too ashamed to admit it. I'm not sure if this conclusion is a reason for optimism – that there's still hope – or, on the contrary, suggests that we should consider the battle lost.

Despite these challenges, there’s still an open door: one that many immigrants from rural areas of Sub-Saharan Africa and Latin America might come through, bringing with them a wealth of knowledge that’s been robbed from us. A few days before my visit to Albacete, I read a book about school textbooks from Spain in the first third of the 20th century. One subject that stood out was *horticulture*. Are we still in time to recover this knowledge?

39. ## Miseries of resilience

In one of her writings, Verónica Sánchez draws attention to the unique situation of the Batanes Islands in the Philippines. This small archipelago, frequently battered by typhoons, serves as a model of what is known as *resilience* — the ability to withstand pressures and problems and to maintain one’s essence in the face of them. As soon as a typhoon has passed and left its destruction, the rebuilding process begins immediately, without waiting for external aid. The local culture is rooted in a blend of autonomy, austerity, and strength, underpinned by community work and mutual aid. Verónica notes that for the island's inhabitants, failing to rebuild their own home or not aiding neighbors in doing so is considered shameful. They maintain a culture that, in all aspects, from food to construction, withstands the tests of the weather and of time.

In the affluent world, there is often talk of resilience, a concept associated with design and urban and territorial development. Cities frequently implement plans for this purpose. These plans blend wealth, safety, tranquility, and comfort on one hand, with the fragility and vulnerability of the inhabitants on the other. These populations depend on macro-systems that are easily unbalanced by minor problems or simple interruptions in supply. The mentioned plans seem destined to fail if, as is common, individual training is neglected. “It's the price of well-being,” Verónica comments, “of an education focused on happiness, of security as a coveted way of life, of reduced effort, of luxury understood as comfort. It is a consequence of the subordination of the individual to infrastructures they are not part of, which create a population dependent on external defense and protection systems.”

Living in a constant storm has enabled the inhabitants of the Batanes to face the tempest. Their culture is inertial and spontaneously resilient, and at the same time, all individuals are resilient, with a clear awareness of the need for mutual aid. How different the situation is in many large cities, which claim the label of resilience even though their inhabitants, unfortunately, have little to do with it.

40. ## Consultants, sheep, and … dogs

The anecdote, which I've heard many times in more or less similar formulations, is well-known. I believe it serves to highlight the superficiality of the knowledge of highly educated people and the arrogance with which they often operate. It also illustrates the awareness of this fact by many humble people who are perceived as foolish.

The story goes that an executive, presumably well-dressed, arrives in a prairie near the French Alps in an expensive car. He meets a shepherd calmly tending to a large flock of sheep. The newcomer, whom we'll assume is from Paris, asks the shepherd whether he could take one of the sheep if, within two minutes, he is able to guess the exact number of sheep in the flock. The shepherd, though hesitant, agrees to the wager. The executive then activates his powerful computer, quickly obtains a satellite image of the flock, and uses an advanced statistical program. Within seconds, he confidently tells the shepherd, "You have 1,634 sheep."

The peasant, stunned, agrees and hands over the animal to the Parisian. Recovering from his surprise, and as the executive is loading his prize into his car, the shepherd makes a counterproposal: "If I can guess your profession, will you return my sheep to me?" The Parisian immediately accepts the challenge. Without delay, the shepherd says: "You are a consultant, educated at the Paris Institute of Advanced Studies." The executive, surprised, confirms this is and naturally asks how the shepherd was so astute. "Very simple," the latter replies. "You came here uninvited, you provided me with information \- the number of sheep in my flock \- that I already knew, and to top it off, as a perfect illustration of your expertise in livestock, you were about to drive off with my dog, which looks nothing like a sheep."

I don't know what stands out more in this story: the natural intelligence of the peasantry, or the congenital stupidity of many of the people who have been educated, indeed, in an institute of advanced studies.

41. ## They shot at clocks

The appearance of clocks in church towers in medieval Europe undoubtedly caused radical changes in the perception of time, particularly in terms of work time. It multiplied mechanisms of control as brutal as what the internet and related tools have enabled in recent decades. It’s not surprising, then, that in the early 19th century, members of the Luddite movement in England destroyed not only machines of the first industrialization, but also clocks that had been installed in factories.  The Luddites saw, with perfect lucidity, that clocks were aberrant forms of control over their own time and their lives. Nor is it surprising that, as Walter Benjamin reminds us, during the July Revolution of 1830 in Paris, clocks were shot at. 

There are many fables that give central importance to time. One of them, told by Anthony de Mello, narrates how animals, gathered in an assembly, started complaining that humans were stealing from them. The cow complained about her milk being taken, the hen about her eggs, the pig about its meat and bacon, the whale about its blubber... The only dissenting voice was that of the snail, who declared: "I have something that, above all else, humans would like to possess. Something they would take from me if they could: time." A well-known saying reminds us how strange we, the inhabitants of the northern part of the planet, are: we have clocks – and yet, we lack time.

42. ##  The GMOs of death.

My colleague Gustavo Duch has written a beautiful text about transgenics and their effects. Here it is. “Upon opening its eyes, that three-century-old tree, two hundred years after its previous blink \- for that is its normal rhythm \- saw everything completely changed. As if by magic, in the blink of an eye \- and this is not metaphorical \- the town it overlooked from its highest branches was completely ruined, as if it had suffered the worst bombardment. The gardens that surrounded it, the mills, the chicken coops, the girls and boys playing, the cows grazing... all of that last image, imprinted on its wooden retina, had been replaced by a vast, monotonous, green field of corn. The tree’s shudder was accompanied by a new sensation, like a sting in its trunk. There was a sign nailed to it, indicating that it was surrounded by GMO corn. It burst into tears of clear sap. (...)"

Submerged in that green and boring world, under the shadow of the old giant, two ephemeral worms, halfway into their lives, conversed while nibbling on leaves. ‘Do you know what I've been told?’ asked the more cheerful of the two. ‘Many years ago, corn was eaten here, but also lettuces, chards, cabbages... and with those foods, we lived much longer than now. In those times, the Sun would hide only to rise again\! Then, in addition to us, there were other animals similar to our great-great-grandparents. They say there were worms that didn’t crawl on the ground like us. They had colorful wings and could fly. Other worms were blind and lived by eating earth, which they expelled. They were only seen when it rained. There were even slimy worms that carried a shell on their backs.’” (...)

GMOs are in our fields and in our diets. In the fields, their latifundist expansion displaces millions of peasant families. Like a reverse King Midas, everything they touch becomes poor. And when they touch crops of native seeds, they infect them with their modified genes, taking them as prisoners. Will they sew inverted triangles on them to suffocate them in concentration camps? Maybe. And in our diets, we ingest them little by little. Potatoes with GMOs, meat with GMOs, popcorn with GMOs, all enriched with their associated pesticides.

And how do we confront this? With a subjugated political class, those who seem to open and close their eyes to the rhythm of those old trees. When they become aware of reality \- if they ever do \- they are left looking dumbfounded, incapable of reacting. (...)".

43. ## Reading Bakunin in the factory

I don't usually read at home. In fact, I only read on public transport – on buses, in the metro, and on trains. However, I read a lot, because in the city where I live, for better or worse, public transport takes up a lot of time. My only real enemy in these places is the noise, which seems to be increasing in our societies. Even so, I think it’s true that if I knew how to drive I would have read a third of the books I have read. Perhaps that's why \- and for other reasons, certainly \- I'm not too fond  of the increasingly faster journeys they insist on offering us.

I am now reminded of a significant and, unfortunately, now forgotten practice of our ancestors, especially those who couldn’t read.  This practice is all the more surprising given that it took place in workplaces, which today seem to be closed off to such activities.  In countries like Cuba and Puerto Rico – and surely the list could be extended – it was common for wage-earners to collectively pay for one of them, man or woman, to read to them from a platform during work. This would happen for a couple of hours each morning and afternoon. The choice of literature was hardly uncontroversial, featuring authors like Bakunin, Kropotkin, Marx... After the reading, the workers would engage in discussions about the ideas presented until the end of the workday.   What a fine job that reader had.  I wish I could have it.

44. ## Anarchists of Temuco

I know of few texts, if any, that better portray the condition of our everyday life. I found it on social media, and it was signed on May 1, 2014, by those who called themselves Anarchists of Temuco. It reads as follows: “To be forced to work will always be to die a little. To enrich others, obey awful bosses, run to not be late, fake smiles, receive starvation wages: all of this kills. To lose moments of love and pleasure, of leisure and creativity, and to forget the ability to imagine completely different tomorrows – this is the sentence imposed on us by the masters of all times and colors. To be an exemplary worker and to endure without protest will never be a sign of pride. We will not be happy slaves. We want to reconquer ourselves, we demand Life. And if something has to die, it is the exploitative order that subjugates us. Health and freedom\!”

What do I see behind those lines? Above all, I see an open challenge to today’s manifestations of voluntary servitude. We end up believing in the unbelievable, and against all reason, we think that work gives us boundless pleasure. As if a subsistence wage, or in some cases, the income that allows us to acquire what we don't need, filled us with happiness. Behind all this, as is well known, lurks a major precondition enabling all of the above: the acceptance of hierarchy and exploitation as innate traits of the human species. And it’s noticeable that the memory of the life that once \- it can be assumed \- made our existence joyful, has disappeared.  Good for you, anarchists of Temuco.

45. ## Hamilton and ordinary people

I understand that when I speak about degrowth or collapse, the image that often comes to listeners' minds is that of highly conscious activists from critical social movements. However, we shouldn’t dismiss the possibility that perceptions like mine may spontaneously resonate with individuals who have never been involved – though they might in the future – with such social movements.

Years ago, a book by an Australian professor named Clive Hamilton was translated into Spanish. The book is titled *Growth Fetish* and it’s essentially an exercise in challenging the supposed virtues of economic growth.  Hamilton discusses a report from the European Union about a phenomenon apparently common in Scandinavian countries. Although these countries' high levels of social security are hardly comparable to other regions, the study focuses on a familiar scenario: an older individual loses their job and faces significant difficulties in finding new employment, along with the accompanying psychological distress. The study concludes that in countries like Norway, Sweden, and Finland, it's quite common to find that with public assistance or a pension – possibly amounting to a quarter of previous earnings when under the yoke of slave labor– and by reducing excessive consumption, one can live much better with less.

I want to emphasize what should be obvious by now: Hamilton isn't talking about highly conscious activists from critical social movements, but about ordinary people who – I repeat – seem willing to conclude, with well-founded arguments, that they can live better with less.

46. ## Technology and traps

The most delicate question I have been asked was at a public event years ago, in Vigo, Galicia.  During my talk, I had expressed the need to critically analyze the nature of the technologies that the system gives us, and concluded that these technologies are not as neutral as they might seem.

A local man took to the floor to explain that he was alive thanks to the very latest technology, which had made possible a delicate surgical operation, and asked if I was opposed to that technology. My response – which I think was as reasonable as it was unimaginative – was that I had nothing against the technology in principle, but, to form a definite judgment, at least two important questions had to be asked. The first was about the social nature of that technology, lest it only benefit, say, 5 percent of the planetary population, in which case we would of course have a problem. The second concerned the ecological-energetic character of the new technology, whether its adoption today hinders access to it for future generations. Had I been more astute, I would’ve added that one ought to scrutinize the political dimensions of the technology, lest its adoption depend on the most aberrant hierarchy.

I should add that the significance of debates about technology vary depending on context.  At the beginning of the 19th century, a very lucid movement emerged in England, Luddism, which sought to destroy many of the technologies of the first industrial revolution. Even then, the moneyed classes told the working class the same thing we hear today: these technologies will allow for a reduction in working hours and significantly increase your well-being. Then, as now, they lied.

47. ## Lifeboats and *carpe diem*

There are many stories about the voyage and sinking of the Titanic.  It’s hardly a coincidence: to varying degrees they’re all about a marvel of technology that was touted as unsinkable, and yet sank to the bottom of the sea on its very first voyage.  

I now recall two of these anecdotes.  The first begins with the question as to why so many human beings died on board the Titanic. The answer is simple: the lifeboats couldn’t accommodate as many people as were on the ship. I’ve often wondered if this could have been remedied – attributing to the question, of course, a metaphorical significance in relation to the impending collapse. The remedy was clear: to dismantle the ship's luxury cabins and use the wood to build new lifeboats. This proposal has much to do with the need to challenge a widely held view suggesting that we should tackle all problems in such a way that no one loses. In my opinion, this a genuine moral scam: those who have placed us in the current scenario, on the brink of an economic, social, and ecological precipice, must indeed take their losses.

The second anecdote is about the attitude of *carpe diem* and its relation, once again, to discourse on collapse. Legend has it that many of the Titanic’s passengers – perhaps the poorest – aware that the ship was inexorably sinking, went on drinking champagne and dancing a waltz. *Carpe diem* urges us to focus exclusively on what is closest in time and space, openly ignoring everything else. Perhaps it's not a very intelligent option.

48. ## Two-ice cream breaks

One of the many common ruses that besiege is to blame others for circumstances that in reality result from our own behavior. In Spain I often hear about the unfair competition of Chinese capital. According to this viewpoint \- whose significance I don’t deny \- Chinese capital disregards considerations of taxes and labor rights, and struggling Spanish businesses cannot compete.

A few years ago I had the opportunity to chat with two Galician businessmen. The first complained bitterly about Chinese competition, saying he was about to throw in the towel. The second, in contrast, confessed that he’d traveled to China many times and was investing most of his resources there. Now the obvious question was: that disloyal capital we call Chinese, isn’t it often our very own capital that, invested in China, seeks to benefit from competition so often described as unfair?

And ultimately, what lies behind the strategy of investing in China? It’s clearly to benefit from much harsher levels of labor exploitation – hard as it is to imagine – than what we see here. Someone told me years ago that a businessman, again from Galicia, had created a significant manufacturing complex in the interior of China. One day, he was told that some employees asked to extend their mid-morning break. They wanted it to be long enough to eat two ice creams instead of just one. The businessman, who was earning substantial profits, was willing to grant them a break long enough to have three ice creams, or even four. But the matter didn’t progress further: the local party organization and the branch of the official union advocated for the dismissal of such indulgent and gluttonous people. No wonder China is so appealing to entire segments of the European business community\!

49. ## Happiness in the eyes of a child

This following story is by the Ecuadorian economist Alberto Acosta. A wealthy man took his young son on a trip to the countryside, intending to show him how humble people live. They spent a full day and night at the farm of a very modest peasant family. On the way back home, the father asked his son what he thought of the trip. The child, who had paid close attention to what he’d seen, answered that he’d really enjoyed the experience.

Wanting to delve a little deeper, the father asked him what exactly he’d learned from the trip.  The child explained. “I saw that we have one dog at home; they have four. We have a pool that goes from one wall to the middle of the garden; they enjoy a creek that never ends. We have imported lamps in the patio; they have the stars. Our patio goes up to the neighbor's house wall; they have a whole horizon for a patio. They have time to talk and be with family; you and mom have to work all the time and I hardly ever see you.” The father was of course left speechless and perplexed, especially when he heard the child’s conclusion: “Thank you, Daddy, for showing me how rich we can be.”

It seems evident that, both in the North and the South, the wealthy and the poor often understand happiness in vastly different ways. Perhaps this is why we shouldn't be surprised that some countries typically labeled as “poor” – often with good reason – actually find themselves among the privileged when assessed using indicators that measure happiness, rather than the figures represented in the colossal deception known as the gross domestic product.

50. ## Of sewing and laundry

In one of her texts, María González Reyes explains the significance of sewing to many women. She describes it in this way: “They gather to sew. Well, actually, they don't gather to sew, they gather for other things. And, incidentally, they sew. If they sew, their husbands don't say anything. If they gather with other women to sew, their husbands don't say anything. Neither does the police. And they sew, incidentally they sew. While they stitch and exchange colored threads, they talk. They talk about their things. Women's things. They talk about economics, about the household, about the world. They talk about politics, about how to reverse unfair situations. They talk about how to take care of those around them. They talk about how to take care of each other. They talk about women's things. And, along the way, they sew.”

María's text reminds me of a tale I've heard a thousand times, encountered in Morocco. It’s about a Spanish non-governmental organization filled with good intentions, aware that the women of a small village had to travel far to the communal laundry area.  The NGO fought to bring water directly to every household. What they didn't realize, of course, was that the distant laundry was the only place where women could talk freely about their own affairs away from the inquisitive gaze of men. Blessed laundry.

The story of the laundry area reminds me of a short tale by Anthony de Mello, titled "The Monkey That Saved a Fish."  Someone asked a monkey why it had taken a fish out of the water and placed it on a tree branch. “I'm saving it from drowning,” replied the monkey... Hell is full of good intentions.

51. ## Our childhood teachers.

If there was one thing I resented when teaching at the university, it was the pressure of time. Because of an increasing productivist impulse, the teaching staff was forced to cram complex subject matter into rigid and, of course, short timeframes. As a result, examples that illustrated theory disappeared, and the chance to debate with the students was curtailed. This environment led to the compression of knowledge that was seldom subject to debate.

And yet, the teachers we remember most fondly from our childhood and adolescence are those who managed to resist the pressure to adhere strictly to a curriculum. I'm thinking of those who would devote all the time in the world to topics they deemed important, with immense patience and care, regardless of the efforts required. They weren't overly concerned with rigidly adhering to a curriculum set by bureaucrats at the Ministry of Education. The lack of progress in this area might be shown by our limited response to what has been called the *slow education* movement.

Some time ago, I heard a journalist argue for a movement of a similar nature: *slow journalism*. I know this might seem almost like a contradiction in terms, as it’s almost impossible to imagine journalism detached from urgency and time pressures. Yet, the foundations of this proposal seem solid: why not invest the time needed to conduct three or four additional interviews, to verify a controversial fact, to get to know the facts firsthand on the ground, or to review all the material with a critical eye?

The only movement of this kind that seems to have prospered somewhat is *slow food*, which encourages us to dedicate the appropriate time to our social life, and to question the origin and journey of the food we consume. However, it's hard not to glance suspiciously at a pamphlet from *Slow Food Bilbao*, clearly promoted by some of the most expensive restaurants in the Basque capital, offering slow food to those who have usually always eaten slowly: the wealthy.

52. ## Two Italian anecdotes, and a Catalan coda

In September 2011, I traveled to Turin, in northern Italy, to participate in a debate about the *15 de Mayo* movement. That short trip left me with two anecdotes that draw from eternal debates about the public sphere, its grandeur, and its miseries.

The first took the form of a conversation between a French colleague and an Italian one, and it wasn't a joke. The French friend said he couldn’t understand how so many Italians could admire or vote for a despicable character like Berlusconi, who, he added, openly hired prostitutes for his nightly parties. The Italian didn’t deny the main point: he readily admitted that many Italians admired Berlusconi, or at least voted for him.  But he then suggested that this admiration might be rooted in something more perverse than what his interlocutor's indignation suggested. He explained that many admired Berlusconi because he paid for prostitutes *with public money*, which – obviously – places us on different terrain.

For my second anecdote, I remember that on the walls of the streets in the center of Turin, there were very eye-catching advertising posters for a local satirical magazine. The main headline read: "The State and the Mafia are splitting up." Following this was a statement from a mafia boss, which stated simply: "They were ruining our reputation." Just like real life.

If you're looking for a response to such miseries, I can think of no better one given by Gustavo Duch. He tells of José Ramón González visiting one of the people I most admire, Bishop Pere Casaldàliga, in Mato Grosso in Brazil, and asking him how to fight hunger. Pere replied something along these lines: "Look, hunger doesn’t wait. The hungry need to be fed. Then comes teaching them to fish. But, above all, above all else, they must know that the river is theirs."

53. ## The statement from the Brazilian news agency

Some time ago, my friend Suso Jares, who has since passed away, made me aware of a striking and very interesting statement released by a Brazilian news agency on a day indelibly marked in memory. I’m talking about September 11, 2001, the day of the attacks in New York and Washington.

The self-explanatory statement was as follows: '35,615 children died of starvation on September 11, 2001\. Victims: 35,615 children (source: Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations, FAO). Location: poor countries. Special television programs: none. Newspaper articles: none. Presidential messages: none. Acts of solidarity: none. Minutes of silence: none. Organized forums: none. Messages from the pope: none. Stock markets: unaffected. Euro: continued as usual. Alert level: zero. Military mobilization: none. Conspiracy theories: none. Main suspects: wealthy countries.'

It’s unnecessary to add that if this happened on September 11, 2011, it happened again on the 12th, on the 13th, reappeared on the 14th... up to today. When we hear from political leaders that the planet’s main problem is what they call *international terrorism*, we have to question the mental balance of so many of them. And about the macabre interests to which they answer.

54. ##  One must be depressed

A text by Matt Haig strikes at the heart, in my view, of many of the irrational elements of wealthy societies. He says: “The world is increasingly designed to depress us. Happiness isn’t very good for the economy. If we were happy with what we had, why would we need more? How do you sell an anti-ageing moisturizer? You make someone worry about aging. How do you get people to vote for a political party? You make them worry about immigration. How do you get them to buy insurance? By making them worry about everything. How do you get them to have plastic surgery? By highlighting their physical flaws. How do you get them to watch a TV show? By making them worry about missing out. How do you get them to buy a new smartphone? By making them feel like they are being left behind. “

Given this, staying still, like the Mexican fisherman, becomes a highly respectable act. It is, after all, about being happy with our normal existence, one not marked by the desire to grow and imitate. Those who lead the economy aren’t interested in us living contentedly in our everyday and very personal chaos. They don’t like us going on being ourselves.

55. ## Zoo delights

I propose to recount two stories concerning our simian brethren.  The first is about what happened with a very well-known albino gorilla named *Copito de Nieve* \[Snowflake\].

According to Salvador Cotelo and Teresa Tristán, *Copito de Nieve* was captured in Africa after his entire family was killed. Purchased in 1966 for a price equivalent to what is now 91 euros, at only two years old, he was placed in a cage at the Barcelona Zoo, in effect receiving a life sentence. As he was a unique specimen, he became the zoo's main attraction. The gorilla's lack of pigmentation, the strength of the Mediterranean sun, and the absence of the forest's shade meant that *Copito de Nieve* would eventually suffer from skin cancer. However, this didn’t seem to bother the zoo's management, which kept him outside despite early signs of skin problems and eye irritation. Although cancer claimed Snowflake's life in 2001, the zoo's business didn’t end there. The animal had been subjected to an ambitious breeding program, intended not to return his offspring to freedom, but rather to exploit new albino gorillas for the sake of profits. Of *Copito*'s twenty offspring, only eight had survived by March 2009\. None of them ever saw the African jungle. All were born, and will die, behind bars.

The second story I mentioned draws from a reflection by Óscar Horta, who invites us to imagine that, for some reason, humans begin to become less intelligent, eventually reaching intellectual capacities similar to those of many chimpanzees today. Suppose also that, in a reverse process, chimpanzees become increasingly intelligent, achieving intellectual abilities similar to those of humans today. And that they decide to hunt humans, confine them in cages, and exploit them for various purposes. How would we judge such behavior?

56. ## Jokes about work

In a book I published years ago, *La parábola del pescador mexicano* \[The Parable of the Mexican Fisherman\], I included some jokes – perhaps as real as life itself – about the world of work. Here are a few of them.

The first joke succinctly goes: “And how's work?” “Bah, it interrupts my morning...” The second is in the same vein: “And how's work?” “Bah, I don’t have time...” A colleague told me that when she used to call a friend on the phone, she would often ask if she was busy. The usual response was: “No, not at all. I’m working.” Apparently, when that friend wasn’t at work, she never answered the phone, which, I believe, is wise. Finally, another colleague told me about an acquaintance who had only one drunken spree in his life; it lasted from age 14 to 45\. He was diagnosed with such severe cirrhosis that he went into retirement due to disability. When asked if he hadn't drunk too much in life, he replied: “I drank just the right amount. If I drink one more glass, I go to the cemetery. If I drink one less, I’d still be working at the factory.”

Though it has little to do with the spirit of these anecdotes, I can't resist including a quote attributed to George Best, the famous Northern Irish footballer. It goes something like this: "The toughest moment of my life was when I decided to stop drinking. Those were twenty horrible minutes..."

57. ## Damned cars

When I speak about degrowth, I try to remember that I’m not a particularly consistent person.  I do, however, have some virtues that stem from my shortcomings. One of these is that I don't know how to drive; therefore, after much deliberation, I decided not to own a car. Someone might wonder if, in the city where I live, I miss having a car. The answer, which I will now justify, is a resounding *no*.

I live in Vallecas, a neighborhood located in the southeast of Madrid. From my house's doorway to a nearby railway station is a five-minute walk. On average, and before I retired, it took me about fifty minutes by train to reach my office at the *Universidad Autónoma*, located in the north of the city. What would have happened if I had a car? Surely, on many days, I might have arrived faster. But other days, given the usual traffic jams in cities, I wouldn't have known exactly what time I would arrive: a bad deal for the diligent students waiting for me in a classroom.   
Secondly, it’s obvious that from an ecological perspective, public transport in general, and the train in particular, is markedly preferable to a car, especially since there’s usually just one person in the latter. Thirdly, the train was much more economical for me. The literature on degrowth is full of exhortations to car owners, urging them to calculate the proportion of their working hours that go into maintaining their vehicles. How many hours of freedom does the car allow, and how many hours of slave labor are necessary to pay for the vehicle, gasoline, taxes, repairs, insurance...? Fourthly, I could read on the train, something that, as far as I know, is best not done by someone driving a car... Finally, my university's station is happily a twelve-minute walk from where I taught my classes: every day, I was forced into basic physical exercise that I wouldn’t have done if I had a car.

I realize there are people who live in certain places or have certain jobs that make the use of a car unavoidable. However, I’m convinced that this isn’t the most common case. Or, to put it another way, that the majority of those who today own cars would live better without them.

58. ## Berger’s friend’s memory

John Berger, in his book *Pig Earth*, recounts a time he was walking through the mountains with a friend, who was about seventy years old at that time. "As we passed by the foot of a high cliff, he told me that a young woman had died there, falling from the pastures while they were mowing the hay. 'Was it before the war?' I asked. 'In 1833,' he replied."

How short our memory is today; a sign \- I suppose \- of how the things we store in our heads lack intensity. Perhaps the elderly do well in losing their recent memory and retaining the distant one. As for me, for many years I lived under the impression that the most recent event was the Spanish Civil War. That perception, an obvious distortion of reality for someone born in 1956, was largely because the results of the war, Franco’s dictatorship, had no interest for me. Today I find myself at a delicate crossroads because the period closest to me, the so-called democratic Spain, also holds little interest for me. It seems sordid, bland, boring, and hypocritical. At the same time, of course, the Civil War feels very, very far away.

Underlying these lapses in memory, I suspect, is the allure I continue to feel for the interwar period and the wars themselves.  A fascination I definitely would not feel had I lived through those times.  As it is, although some recollections of Galician expressions I heard at home as a young child date back to the early 19th century War of Independence, my memory is much less firm, much less intense, than that of Berger's friend. And I regret that.

59. ## Nono and his stories

*Les aventures de Nono* \[The Adventures of Nono\], a book written by the French author Jean Grave and published in the early 20th century, was very influential in the Spanish anarchist world before the civil war of 1939\. Here, I recall a couple of paragraphs from its pages because they highlight that over a century ago, there were some who already were clearly aware of something important: schools shape our minds according to criteria determined by the rules of private profit and usury.

Nono \[a child of nine\] was asked to dictate a math problem.  He chose one that he remembered from his time in school.   It was about a merchant who, after buying many meters of cloth for a given amount, asked himself how much he should sell it for to obtain a certain profit. The teacher, named *Solidaria* \[Solidary\], responded that yes, the problem was well constructed, but its formulation pointed to selfish practices worth discussing, as they were clearly rooted in the intention to exploit other human beings. And to help Nono understand, she suggested a kind of alternative formulation as follows: “Given that a man possesses so many pieces of cloth and can make so many dresses from each one, how many friends can he please by giving a dress to each of them?”

Kissing Nono affectionately, Solidaria added: “Perhaps you’re too young to see the difference, but when you grow up and can compare, you’ll understand.” If only someone had taken note, over the last hundred years, when writing many of the textbooks that are fed to young people. And if only, occasionally, the owner of so many pieces of cloth in these books were a woman.

60. ## Barrett’s chickens

Few texts better portray the vices of private property than this masterful tale by Rafael Barrett, the Spanish anarchist who flourished in Argentina, Brazil, Uruguay, and especially Paraguay. Its lines depict the unhappiness that, in a curious paradox, stems from the accumulation of goods, the deterioration \- and then, the debasement \- of human relationships, and ultimately, a lacerating insecurity that makes one long for the peaceful times when one owned neither roosters nor hens.

"When I owned nothing more than my cot and my books, I was happy. Now I own nine hens and a rooster, and my soul is troubled. Property has made me cruel. Whenever I bought a hen, I tied it to a tree for two days to impose my domicile on it, erasing from its fragile memory the love for its old residence. I mended the fence of my yard to prevent the escape of my birds and the invasion of both four- or two-footed foxes.  I isolated myself, fortified the border, drew a diabolical line between my neighbor and me. I divided humanity into two categories; me, the owner of my hens, and the others, who could take them from me. I defined crime. The world filled up with potential thieves for me, and for the first time, I cast a hostile glance over the fence."

"My rooster was too young. The neighbor's rooster jumped the fence and courted my hens, making life bitter for my rooster. I chased the intruder away with stones, but my hens jumped the fence and laid eggs at the neighbor's house. I claimed the eggs, and my neighbor grew to hate me. Since then, I saw his face over the fence, his inquisitive and hostile look, identical to mine. His chickens crossed the fence and ate the soaked corn I had set aside for mine. The neighbor's chickens seemed criminal to me. I chased them, and blinded by rage, killed one. The neighbor attributed enormous importance to the attack. He refused monetary compensation. He gravely retrieved the corpse of his chicken, and instead of eating it, showed it to his friends, starting the village legend of my imperialist brutality. I had to reinforce the fence, increase surveillance, raise, in a word, my war budget. The neighbor has a dog ready for anything; I plan to acquire a revolver."

"Where is my old tranquility? I am poisoned by distrust and hatred. The spirit of evil has taken hold of me. I was once a man. Now I am a property owner..."

61. ## A bookstore that confuses value and price

Not long ago, I received a message from a woman who worked in a bookstore located in a provincial capital in Spain. The bookstore was to celebrate its fiftieth anniversary the following year. They wanted to commemorate it with various events, and asked whether I’d be willing to visit for a discussion with a local journalist about the current international political situation

I replied immediately, saying I would be delighted to attend, as long as we could agree, of course, on a date for the debate. My correspondent took her time to respond. A few weeks later she sent a very brief message, asking about my fee for participating. I quickly replied that if they had money to pay me, I would gladly accept it, but if they didn't, I would still attend the bookstore's half-century celebration.

I never heard back from the woman. While there may be many explanations for this silence, one stands out to me: my correspondent realized that someone who didn't charge for participating in a debate wasn't high-profile enough to take part in the commemorative events of a prestigious bookstore. As we know, Antonio Machado advised us not to confuse value with price. Too bad I didn't ask for three thousand euros.

62. ## The earnings of the world’s best violinist

A story that reminds me a little of the bookstore anecdote is a juicy tale by Argentine writer César Aira. An immensely wealthy woman who, wanting to add prestige to a dinner party, decided to entertain her guests with violin music and set out to hire the world's best violinist for the occasion, whom she assured she could afford.

Someone suggested the name of the violinist, reputed to be the finest in the world – his name and surname were unmistakably German – and the dinner hostess promptly called him. The violinist, presumably quite matter-of-fact, said he didn't perform at private events as his fees were very high and he doubted anyone could meet them. The lady, undeterred, asked how much he charged, and the violinist quoted a supposedly unreachable figure of ten thousand dollars. "No problem. I'll expect you tonight," the wealthy woman replied, but she immediately added a condition: "You will dine in the kitchen with the servants and under no circumstances may you speak to my guests." "In that case," the musician retorted, "my fee changes." "No problem," the dinner hostess agreed. "Tell me how much it is." "Two thousand dollars," the violinist stated.

While I understand this story often appears in psychology books, I find it fits better in textbooks on sociology or, even better, ethics. The violinist preferred the company and conversation of the servants and was therefore willing to charge much less. Or even, why not, play for free.

63. ## The three phrases of 2017

At the end of 2017, I decided to compile the most striking statements that I had come across on social media over the previous months. My list included three that, to my mind, reflect the miserable moment we’re in regarding power, social relations, and knowledge.

The first appeared in a cartoon featuring two elderly individuals. One said to the other something along the lines of: "Who would have thought that our most revolutionary commitment would consist simply in going on living, so that our children and grandchildren may also live, thanks to our pensions?" 

The second I owe to my friend Felipe Zapico, who very aptly asserted that "if banks had to return all that they have stolen, we would find out how much it was." Indeed, banks always win, and when we think we have come out victorious, in the end they walk away with the ball. Why is that? What order of things allows it?

The third, finally, was a succinct phrase that illustrates how by paying attention to important matters,  we disregard others of much greater significance. It went more or less like this: "You, who are so outraged by corruption, just wait till you hear about surplus value." Corruption, while undeniably significant, is far less so compared to the daily exploitation of billions of human beings. But – you see – about surplus value, poor Marx, we speak very little. Or not at all.

64. ## Spanish libertarians

I have often thought – and I will return to this in the epilogue of this book – that the fascination that we continue to feel for pre-1939 Spanish anarchism is rooted in a specific reality.  In the anarchist world, two distinct yet complementary impulses spontaneously converged, one of a pre-capitalist character and the other of an anti-capitalist nature.

The first was linked to the rural origins of many of these individuals, and was materialized in the non-ideological and experiential preservation of collective work practices and behaviors, rooted in solidarity and mutual support. The second impulse was caused by anarchist ideology, which, proudly anti-capitalist, gave impetus to many of the collective and solidarity-oriented elements that the pre-capitalist impulse provided.

The combination of these two elements had many fortunate consequences. For example, it enabled the suggestive experiment of the collectivizations that took shape during the Spanish Civil War. They emphatically demonstrated that it’s possible to manage a complex society without relying on entrepreneurs, foremen, and bureaucrats. But it also made possible a strike in which a significant part of the proletariat of Zaragoza showed their solidarity with a German communist leader imprisoned by the Nazis. Note that I'm not talking about a strike motivated by labor issues. It wasn't about wage increases or demands for reduced working hours.  The reduction of working hours was, however, behind the demands for respect of religious holidays made during the civil war by many anarcho-syndicalist workers, who understood with unyielding rigor that a revolution that did not entail a reduction in working hours was unimaginable. I suspect that behind this lay the lingering influence of many codes characteristic of pre-capitalist societies.

65. ## Indigenous peoples and self-restraint

Here is a collection of texts that emerged from indigenous communities, representing a genuine claim to the land, a claim clearly opposed to the miseries characterizing the Western man's relationship with it. The first notion that I believe inspires these texts is *self-restraint*.

Norbert S. Hill reminds us of the behavior of many of the indigenous peoples of North America: "When we Indians kill meat, we eat it. When we dig roots, we make small holes. When we build houses, we also make small holes. When we burn grass, we don’t ruin the burned earth. We shake the acorns and pine cones. We don’t cut down trees. We only use dead wood. But the white man plows the ground, cuts down trees, kills everything. The white man doesn’t pay attention. How can the spirit of the land and the white man get along?"

I have emphasized elsewhere that Eugene P. Odum has noted, with provocative intent, how some Indian cultures located in what we now call the United States show characteristics of a “mature” ecosystem: “protection as against production, stability as against growth, quality as against quantity. In Pueblo societies a kind of ultimate democracy is practiced. Plants and animals are also people, and, through certain rituals and dances, are given a place and a voice in the political discussions of the humans: They are ‘*represented*.’”

Pierre Clastres has written that if primitive humans didn’t maximize the efficiency of their activity, it wasn’t because they didn’t know how, but rather because they didn’t want to: they didn’t aspire to merely profit from such activities. Strikingly, in the languages of many indigenous communities in the Amazon, there is no word for "work". Time is spent laughing, talking, celebrating. When a cabin needs to be built, it’s built. When hunting is needed for food, they hunt. But these activities aren’t unnecessarily prolonged.

66. ## The frog does not drink up the pond in which it lives

"The frog does not drink up the pond in which it lives," goes a proverb. There is no better phrase to portray a balanced survival strategy that thinks clearly and lucidly about the future.

Eduardo Galeano has reminded us that in certain areas of the Amazon – we travel now to the southern part of the American continent – the intelligence of indigenous agricultures is clearly on show. According to Galeano, the soils there are fertilized without decomposition, nourished by countless small pieces of ceramics scattered by the indigenous people, returning to the earth what comes from the earth. In this way, the land is constantly regenerated, "from time to time, from hand to hand."

Finally, I will let another indigenous person speak – I return now to the north of the continent \- who, in conversation with the powerful white man, portrays with clarity their preferences and values:  “Our land is more valuable than your money. It will last forever. It will not even perish in the flames of fire. As long as the sun shines and the waters flow this land will be here to give life to men and animals. We cannot sell the lives of humans and animals, therefore we cannot sell this land... You can count your money and burn it within the nod of a buffalo’s head, but only the Great Spirit can count the grains of sand and the blades of grass of these plains. As a gift to you, we will give you everything we have that you can take with you; but the land, never."

67. ## Let’s draft our own Constitution

I can't resist including here this story that – I believe – illustrates how the Western world always tries to impose its rules and principles without respecting the decision-making autonomy and the sound judgment of those it deems inferior. I heard it from a colleague, Marta Llorente, many years ago.

The story is set in the Amazon, perhaps a century ago. A Brazilian official was tasked with explaining a typical liberal constitution, like the one prevailing in the country at the time, to members of an indigenous community in the rain forest. He met with the leader of that community beforehand, who made him a proposal: "Explain to me what a Constitution is, and I, who better understand the mentality of my people, will transmit it to them without a problem."

They did just that, and the official soon observed that the formula was happily effective. The community leader had understood very well what a constitution is, and had explained its fundamental features in a very graphic way, saving the representative of the Brazilian government a cumbersome and presumably inefficient task. Everything was going perfectly until the official heard the last sentence from his translator: "You have now seen what a constitution is. And you have verified that it’s something beneficial. Let's get to work on our own constitution." A good counter, in every sense, to the colonial convulsion induced by the supposedly neutral laws we promote across the planet.

68. ## Arendt and the mortgage-afflicted 

My friend S., petite and of fragile health, has dedicated her life to defending just causes. A few years ago, as an activist of the PAH \- the *Plataforma de Afectados por la Hipoteca* \[Platform for People Affected by Mortgages\] in Madrid \- she requested a meeting with the director of a bank branch. The situation at hand was an elderly woman whom the bank wanted to evict from her home. The woman was offered several alternative apartments; some were already occupied by other people, and the remaining one had a hole in the roof through which one could see the stars.

I suppose S. approached the meeting as a sort of bureaucratic obligation. The fact is that during the course of the meeting she took the opportunity to recommend a book by Hannah Arendt titled *Eichmann in Jerusalem* to the inhumane bank branch manager, who was unyielding. Arendt's text is concerned with what she calls the *banality of evil*, and does so through the figure of one of the Nazi hierarchs most directly involved in the Jewish Holocaust. The book reminds us how many people in those circumstances hid behind the *duty of obedience,* and that any one of us could be ensnared by such an infernal machinery.

I assume that S. left the bank with little hope that the elderly woman would ultimately have a decent home. A few days later, to her surprise, my friend received a phone call from the bank branch manager. He had purchased the book, read it, was persuaded by the thesis, and had decided to allocate a decent home to the elderly lady. Arendt would have risen from her grave had she known that one of her works had evidently served a purpose.

Some time after I learned of this story, I met S. one afternoon.  She told me there had been a sequel to the event. One day, she and a friend had entered a bar in a town near Madrid. A gentleman was looking at her intently. When she went to pay, she discovered that this man had paid their bill. She approached him to ask why, and the good man introduced himself as the former director of the bank branch. He had left the bank and opened a bar. I suspect that the cause of such a change was not really the reading of Hannah Arendt, but the persistence of my friend S. Faith moves mountains, and above all, it moves people.

69. ## Talking is living

I have heard this story several times, in somewhat different versions, but in all cases it’s set somewhere in Sub-Saharan Africa. It could hardly be otherwise.

The protagonist is a peasant who, out of necessity and poverty, traveled many kilometers once a week to the market in a larger town. Surprisingly, and for reasons that will soon become clear, he enjoyed spending a good number of hours there. So much so that he readily woke up early and left after nightfall.

One morning, very early, he laid out on a cloth on the ground the few goods he had managed to gather: two bananas, a couple of tomatoes, three apples, and four onions. Soon after, a tourist appeared and asked how much he wanted for everything. Initially, the peasant, stunned, didn't know what to say. But then he named a price which the tourist accepted. The tourist took out the money and prepared to take the bananas, tomatoes, apples, and onions. But the peasant quickly reacted and refused the tourist's money, who couldn't understand what was happening. Then he got a thorough explanation. "If you buy all this from me, there's no reason for me to stay here. I do come to earn some money I need. But for me, as important as that money is the interaction with the people who pass by and with those who also offer their products. I think I could live without the money I get here, but I could never do without those conversations. If you want to take a banana or a tomato, I'll gladly give it to you, but I can't let you take away everything else."

70. ## Degrowth architecture

Verónica Sánchez – I've mentioned her before – is an architect specializing in the design of field hospitals in areas afflicted by wars or disasters. She works in Madrid at a studio that operates within the orbit of the degrowth movement. Though it might seem hard to imagine, it’s possible to develop an architecture that challenges the whirlwind construction of paraphernalia, and destruction of the natural environment, that indelibly marks the everyday practice of the discipline.

One day she told me a story, which I believe I'm not distorting too much, about her studio entering a competition in a Swedish municipality. The challenge was to determine what should be done with a space on the outskirts of town. The Madrid studio won the competition with a groundbreaking proposal: to do nothing.  That is, to leave the space reasonably as it was, untouched; or alternatively – I imagine – to remove any unfortunate elements that human intervention might have left on the land. However, it seems the outcome was contested by the group that placed second, who in the end, unsurprisingly, got the decision reversed and succeeded in pushing forward – I suppose again – a wonderful and elitist development.

When I heard this story, it reminded me of many discussions I've heard in the anarchist world.  I believe it’s quite common in that world to view the State as an intrinsically physical apparatus that needs to be destroyed. We are to grab picks and shovels to dismantle the buildings – horrendous, by the way – of the *Nuevos Ministerios* \[New Ministries\] in Madrid. Although I have often replied that unfortunately the matter is more complex, since we carry the State in our heads, there’s still an argument for deleting the unhealthy consequences of the actions of our species, as was suggested to the authorities of the Swedish municipality.  And for doing the same with what's inside some heads.

71. ##   Gadgets for entertainment

Irene, my mother, passed away in early 2018, at the age of 95\. Although she rarely complained, the last months of her life were unequivocally tough. She was bedridden, blind and very deaf. Naturally I asked her several times if she was bored. And she always gave me the same answer: she relied on her formidable memory to imagine entering Lugo, her hometown, or A Coruña, the city of her loves. She remembered – saw, really – the rigorously organized stores she passed by – the haberdashery, the stationery shop, the shoe store, the grocery... and retained a precise memory of the benches in the Relleno park in A Coruña, around the statue of Curros.

What fascinated me the most about the several times she shared this narrative wasn't her elephantine memory. After all, those who had survived very hard times must naturally be endowed with special capabilities. Perhaps they had no choice but to rely on a memory we lack, because we believe we don't need it. What most fascinated me was realizing how the majority of her generation could “entertain” themselves \- I put the verb in quotes because it seems insufficient \- simply by moving their heads and delving into what’s in them. How different are the times we live in. Today, to entertain ourselves \- this time I don't put the infinitive in quotes \- we need an endless array of gadgets: the mobile phone, television, gaming console, movies, tablet... Yet, I doubt we enjoy ourselves more than our ancestors. We get bored.

72. ## They were requested a lot.

Rafael Azcona was, among many other things, the scriptwriter for several films by Luis García Berlanga. He liked to tell a story that I became aware of through an account by Jacobo Fitz-James Stuart’s, which in turn appeared in a newspaper article by Fernando Esteve.

It seems that in the mid-20th century, Azcona traveled by car from Madrid to Zaragoza with some friends. They stopped at an inn where, according to one of Azcona's companions, they served very tasty muffins – I assume that our man and his colleagues enjoyed the muffins. Some time later, knowing that an acquaintance was going to take the same route, Rafael recommended that particular inn.  When he later asked about the visit – "weren't the muffins good?" he surely asked – his interlocutor succinctly replied that the inn no longer served muffins. Faced with Azcona's incredulity, he explained that he had asked about them, of course, and the waiter, who probably had to be prodded for an answer, explained dryly: "We no longer make them. Too many people were asking for them."

Fitz-James Stuart suggests that the waiter had a unique way of understanding business. Esteve corroborates this in his article, rightly estimating that this anonymous hero was the antithesis of the idolized entrepreneur of our days. His perception of economic facts was indelibly marked by a long workday standing behind a sordid bar, with no other conversation other than what was necessary to serve coffee and muffins. Wouldn't it be much more reasonable to forego the meager profit derived from selling these muffins in favor of a shorter, calmer workday, and warmer social relations? Wasn’t it more logical, as Esteve points out again, to renounce his overwhelming condition as a *homo economicus* to regain the status of *homo* without adjectives? The story, and with it the explanation, reminds me of something Arturo de Nieves told me. While walking through a Galician city, Arturo came across a bar with a sign on the door that read: "Closed due to tiredness, not for rest."

73. ## There are no cinemas in Albania

I have the impression that in the coming years, many processes, in the context of a foreseeable collapse, will experience rapid acceleration. As a result, I suspect that some of the concepts that have seemingly provided us with certainties in understanding the world will cease to be useful – or, at least, will need to undergo deep revision.

I often remember a story I heard – I believe from Francisco Veiga – at the end of the 1990s. A movie was filmed whose main plot line was that Albania had declared war on the United States. In a gathering of intellectuals in France, in which an Albanian was present, a discussion took place on whether the American film could be shown in Albania or if, on the contrary, it hurt deep national feelings. While some thought the former and others maintained the latter, the Albanian remained silent. When the conversation died down, however, his voice was heard. He simply said: "In Albania, there are no cinemas." The enthralling discussion about the supposed effects of a film on national feelings hinged on a material element that was strikingly missing.

In my view, it's likely that in the coming years we'll find ourselves repeatedly asserting that in Albania there are no cinemas. We’ll need to do this, for example, when someone assumes that new technologies will emerge that will magically solve problems that today seem insurmountable. Given the prospect of collapse, we’ll have to ask where the energy will come from – where the cinemas will come from – that will allow these technologies to operate.

74. ## Death and life, darkness and light.

There are two texts by María González Reyes that faithfully portray, for women, death and darkness – the one \-, and life and light – the other. The first is about women who work as live-in employees in houses that aren’t theirs. “There are many hard things about our job. Some are small details, like not drying your body after a shower with your own towel, or with a friend's towel who lent you a room to live. You dry off with the towel of someone who pays you a salary on which you couldn’t survive a month in the city. You always dry off with the work towel, at work, because you live at work. I know it seems trivial to you, drying your face with a towel that belongs to the person who pays you a salary they would never accept for themselves. It touches your skin. It takes away the water. You can't have a life project if you live in a house that’s your job. A house full of towels that are piled in the cupboards. One day. Another day. The same towel in the same cupboard in the same house. The days. And I’m that face without its own towel to dry off. I’m that face that looks into a round mirror after wiping the water from the face. There’s no intimacy for live-in workers. There’s loneliness, in the smallest room of the house, in the most secluded area. There’s loneliness. There’s no life project because there are no life projects without other people. There’s no joint project for two. There’s no partner. There’s no possibility of children. There’s no everyday life beyond who pays you. A miserable salary. There’s no sex with another person if you live at your work. Live-in workers also like sex. I don't know if you’d thought about that.”

The second text I mentioned is about a woman who spoke up at a public event. "I'm going to tell you," she said, "what my grandmother used to say. My grandmother spoke of the economy of open hands. She would look at me and say: ‘Go fetch me an avocado in the yard. But count with your hand. One avocado has to be for the earth, so another avocado can grow. The second is for the birds, so they can also continue living. The third is for the neighbors, in case any of them needs it. The fourth is for us to cook our recipe. And the fifth is for the one who comes.’" The grandmother – María adds – explained in a very graphic way how life should be at the center.

75. ## The supermarket piglet

In a book titled *Un paso adelante en defensa de los animales* \[A Step Forward in Defense of Animals\], Óscar Horta proposes an exercise for the imagination. Suppose – he says – that we have broken a leg, we can't go out to shop, so we place an order at the supermarket. The order consists of vegetables, rice, legumes, and pork. When they knock on the door, however, we’re informed that because there’s a strike at the slaughterhouse, they can't provide us with the processed meat we requested. Instead, they bring us a piglet that licks the delivery person’s hand and tries to play with her. Along with the piglet there’s a large knife and an apron. We can stick to the vegetables, of course, but if we want meat, we’ll have no choice but to slaughter the piglet, which will of course resist the unspeakable. In that situation, what would we do?

Horta enters into an interesting discussion, questioning whether, if all humans became vegans, there would be enough plant products to guarantee our nutrition. Is it possible that veganism isn't a viable alternative, regardless of our commendable effort to defend animal rights? The answer here is reasonably simple: animals exploited for our food must, of course, be fed. If instead of allocating legumes, cereals, and other vegetables to the feeding of these animals for the meat industry, we used them to feed humans, wouldn't we have more than enough food? Wouldn't we significantly reduce the emissions of substances that, because of the meat industry, are at the origin of climate change? And ultimately, wouldn't we end the immense suffering that so many animals endure?

76. ## Wang, the free peasant

Here is a story about a Chinese peasant, narrated by Santiago Alba Rico. It goes like this: "Wang, a poor farmer who could barely feed his family, one day found a large earthenware jar and brought it home. While cleaning it, his brush fell inside, and the jar suddenly filled with brushes: brushes upon brushes, and for every one that Wang took out, another magically appeared inside. For a few months, the Wang family lived off selling brushes in the market, and their situation, while still not comfortable, improved significantly. But one day, while taking brushes out of the jar, Wang dropped in a coin, and then the jar filled with coins: coins upon coins that reproduced and multiplied as Wang took them out by the handful.

The Wang family thus became the wealthiest in the village, and with so many coins produced by the jar and with the family so occupied, the Wangs assigned the grandfather, already useless for the pleasures of the world, the task of continuously shoveling them out and accumulating them in a corner, mountains and mountains of gold that increased and renewed at a rate that no spending could surpass. For a few more months, the Wang family was happy. But the grandfather was old and weak, and one day, leaning over the jar, he fainted, fell inside, and died there. And then the jar immediately filled with dead grandfathers: corpse after corpse that had to be taken out and buried with no hope of finishing the task, endless lifeless old men who kept appearing from the inexhaustible bottom of the jar. Thus, the Wang family spent all their money and the rest of their lives burying their dead grandfather a million times over.

What must we say about greed, and the sense of limits, especially when these show up in a closed environment like that of a family that doesn’t look outward, where accumulation and happiness are confused. How hard it seems to be to break free from all this, in China as here.

77. ## From Marx and Engels to China

Many years ago, I came across an article \- don't ask me the title, author, or publication \- that gave me a lot to think about. The forgotten author of this text began by noting that Marx’s and Engels’s forecasts regarding the proletariat in Western Europe, made in the second half of the 19th century, proved to be inaccurate. The German, French, and English proletariat didn’t carry out the revolution that Marx and Engels hoped for.  With a touch of irony, we might say that the European proletariat went into a supermarket and decided to stay, seduced by all its products.

However, the reflection didn’t end there. Our author invited us to consider our present, in which the proletariat as a class has been disappearing almost everywhere. The "almost" is justified partly because a very large human group that resists being labeled as “proletarian” has been accumulating in the coastal areas of China, under conditions that strongly resemble those of Western Europe in the second half of the 19th century. Why not imagine – the article asked – that the Chinese proletariat might eventually carry out the revolutionary task that Marx and Engels assigned to their German, French, and English contemporaries?

What interests me about this argument isn't so much its supposed accuracy, but rather what it points to. Expressed a couple of decades ago, such a thesis would have elicited nothing but pitying smiles. Today, however, in the uncertainty stemming from our difficulty in understanding the world we live in and in foreseeing its future, we have no choice but to consider it seriously. It might just be one of the surprises the aforementioned future holds for us.

78. ## Ubuntu\! Ubuntu\!

It's another story that's been told a thousand times, each with its own variation. In one of the many versions, the initial protagonist was an anthropologist immersed in his fieldwork, seeking to study the customs of an African community. One day, he decided to propose a game to the children of the village where he was living. The anthropologist, having bought a good assortment of fruits and candies in the city, placed them in a basket under a tree. He gathered the children and gave a simple instruction: whoever reached the tree first would win all the fruits and candies.

When the anthropologist signaled the start of the race, a surprising situation unfolded: at first, no one moved. Soon after, the children held hands and walked together towards the tree. After encircling the basket, they sat down and began to distribute the fruits and candies among themselves. When the anthropologist asked why they acted this way, knowing that the fastest runner could have taken everything, they replied in unison, "Ubuntu\! Ubuntu\! How could the one who ran the fastest be happy when all the other children would be sad?"

It's not easy to measure the immense damage that the prevailing individualism in Western societies has caused among us. Aren't those children, who spontaneously concluded that one can hardly be happy when surrounded by the world's many ills, absolutely right?

79. ## A man, a fishing rod, a river …

My friend Enrique Falcón is responsible for this epistolary story that portrays how difficult it is to get ahead in a society where the powerful only create obstacles to the unfolding of life.

"Letter from January 21: Today I found a man by the dock who is starving...

Letter from February 8: Remember the man I told you about? Raquel and I have decided to go to the dock once a day and give him some fish to eat.

Letter from February 15: We continue to visit him (...) with daily food, but we fear the day we can't go there and the man at the dock will be left without his fish. He is grateful to us. His cheeks are starting to regain color. He seems a bit stronger. Some nights we've invited him to dinner with the family. He is quite shy.

Letter from March 10: Raquel and I have decided to buy him a fishing rod. We plan to give him a comprehensive and detailed manual on fishing tackle and techniques (...). They say the river is full of fish. We believe that in a short time he’ll be able to fish for himself. He could get food on his own and maybe some money from selling the surplus catch.

Letter from March 23: Problems arise. Learning to fish has not enabled the man at the dock to do without us. He needs a license and some other papers to fish from the river. The permits cost a good amount of money, and he has none to pay. We found out that the river's exploitation is exclusive to the municipality and you can't fish there without the proper paperwork.

Letter from March 25: More problems: the local police caught the man at the dock fishing without a license and now he is detained. The bail (...) is not exactly cheap. We will try to cover it for him. People in the town are saying that he tried to take advantage of the community, that he is a thief and that he deserved it.

Letter from April 29: Another complication, and this one seems serious. Did I tell you that the man at the dock got out of jail and got the necessary fishing permits? Well, they are useless now: the plastic factory in the town, upstream, has polluted the waters and all the fish are dead. Not a single one is left and the sight is desolate. They say there will be no fishing for about ten years or so. The industry will pay an astronomical fine (which it can easily afford), will buy some waste filters, and will continue producing...

Letter from April 30: The man at the dock is starving again."

80. ## Credits and computers

Returning to the story of Uncle Arsenio, I now add a special dimension in connection with the wonderful technologies imposed on us.

John Berger recounts in his book "Pig Earth" that a farmer was offered a loan to acquire a tractor. The cost of the latter was equivalent to the annual yield of ten cows, each producing 2,500 liters of milk – in other words, a fortune. Once the farmer came into possession of the tractor, he was told that to get the most out of it, he needed to acquire various complementary gadgets, forcing him to apply for another loan. These machines in turn required more add-ons, triggering new loans, so the farmer found himself increasingly indebted. In the end, he was forced to sell his business, and the cows, the milk, the tractor, and the auxiliary machines all disappeared.

Berger's story reminds me of another one included in David Anisi's *Cuentos Económicos* \[Economic Tales\].  Anisi tells of a writer who had the healthy habit of capturing his stories on beautiful white paper. One of his admirers suggested that he modernize and use floppy disks to save and send his stories. Our writer bought a computer and spent time learning how to use it. It also took him a lot of effort to transfer the content of his creations onto floppy disks. Meanwhile, he of course stopped writing. When the task was finished, he discovered that the writing program he was using had become outdated and the floppy disks were no longer useful. Our author had to buy a new computer and embark on the tedious process of learning and transferring again, which once more took up his time and prevented him from writing.

As you might imagine, the devices on which he eventually managed to save his stories soon stopped working, so this poor soul had to buy an even more modern computer... After fifteen years of creative drought, the writer earn from a fig seller in the port of Izmir, who, at the end of his work, sat with friends and told the most beautiful tales.

81. ## The truth of the social sciences

Elias Milojević, my Serbian-Chilean friend who sadly passed away, once told me a story – there are several versions out there – that humorously accounts for the precariousness of much of our knowledge. In a Midwestern American city, known for its technological advancement, there's an indigenous community on the outskirts. This community blends modern developments with ancestral customs.

In August, the leaders of the indigenous community approach the shaman, the religious leader, to ask what the next winter will be like.  The shaman replies confidently that it will be extremely harsh, with extreme temperatures, frequent frosts, torrential rains, and constant snow. The community members immediately start chopping wood like madmen. The conversation repeats in the same terms in September, October, and November, so they continue chopping wood breathlessly. December arrives, and the shaman gives the same response about the severity of the upcoming winter. Once the community leaders have left, however, he begins to doubt and decides to make an appointment at the local weather station.

The director of the station receives him and, when asked about the condition of the upcoming winter, responds, to the shaman's relief, that it will be extremely harsh. The shaman asks how they know this and ventures to suggest that they compare historical data sets from satellites and the records they have had for many years. The station director interrupts him and says, "Not at all, my friend\! We know because the indigenous people have been chopping wood like madmen for five months"... This is how the knowledge of social sciences is often forged, I'm afraid.

Not long ago, I came across a similar story. A person who was impressed by the accuracy of weather forecasts made by a New York institute decided to ask how they were made. The head of the institute explained that it was located in the tallest building in the United States. When asked if the measuring devices worked better because of this, the head said no, that wasn't the reason, and had the person accompany him to the rooftop. Once there, the director of the center showed them the smoke signals emitted by the Dakota indigenous people.

82. ## Western civilization

I really like a text from Eduardo Galeano from his book *Patas Arriba* \[Upside Down\]. It goes like this: "From the point of view of the owl, the bat, the bohemian, and the thief, twilight is breakfast time. Rain is a curse for the tourist and good news for the farmer. From the native's point of view, it is the tourist who is picturesque. From the point of view of the Indians of the Caribbean islands, Christopher Columbus, with his feathered hat and his red velvet cape, was a parrot of dimensions never seen before."

Galeano himself notes that the first time he was kicked out of class was when, as a very young child, the teacher explained that Balboa, the Spanish *conquistador*, had seen both the Pacific and Atlantic Oceans at the same time a peak in Panama. When the teacher added that he was the first man to have seen both seas at once, young Eduardo dared to ask if the indigenous people were blind…

Clearly, we’re the ones who don’t see. We've built our civilization on the foundation of forgetting, in the best case, and exterminating, in the worst, the people we take to be different. It seems that Gandhi was once asked what he thought of Western civilization. Quite rightly, and with a very appropriate use of the conditional, he replied: "I think it would be an excellent idea."

83. ## Stable bankruptcy in Gaza

About thirty years ago, I met the Uruguayan ethologist Eduardo Gudynas in Nicaragua. As is often the case, It was an era of hyperinflation in his country. One fine day, I ventured to ask him how they coped with the fact that bread cost ten in the morning and eleven in the afternoon. He replied, with thinly veiled irony, that people in Uruguay lived in a state of stable bankruptcy. It was undoubtedly bankruptcy, but since it unfolded in a predictable manner, the mind ended up adapting to conditions that eventually seemed normal. I suppose we may deduce that the people of Uruguay didn’t see reality as we often do in the North, marked by ups and downs we call crises and booms. Such are the advantages of having a stable bankruptcy.

When writing  a book titled *Collapse*, published in 2016,  I often thought that the concept of collapse, in its everyday use in the Northern countries, has a fairly ethnocentric character. We understand the concept because we assume we’re not yet in it, so we compare our present situation to a possible future. On the other hand, explaining collapse to a girl born in the Gaza Strip seems to me extremely difficult: that girl doesn't have the possibility of comparison, since her entire life has been a genuine collapse. We need to remember that the collapse I'm talking about isn't just something that might happen tomorrow: it's the present reality of hundreds of millions of human beings.

84. ## The price of potatoes

There's a very old cartoon by Mingote, from the Franco years, which depicts a poor person approaching a wealthy individual and somewhat awkwardly saying: "I believe they've raised the price of potatoes for you." The misery was such that the increase in question didn’t actually affect the poor since these people couldn't even afford potatoes.

I also recall something my mother once told me. In the days when the euro was being introduced, she went down to the shop next to her house and overheard a conversation between the shopkeeper and an elderly lady. The latter asked, "And how much do you say a loaf of bread costs now?" "Thirty cents," replied the shopkeeper. Wide-eyed, the old lady said excitedly, "Give me six loaves." Her memory of many years ago, from the era of the peseta marked by countless hardships, advised buying what clearly wasn't needed, to take advantage of a supposed offer.  But one had to put oneself in the place, and in the life, of the old lady.

I would like to conclude with a Jewish joke: A man says to another, 'If I had been Rothschild, I would have had more money than him.' 'How come?' replies his interlocutor. 'Because I would have continued giving private lessons in the afternoon and would have earned some extra money'... How difficult it is to weigh the economic options and perceptions of people. However obscure they may seem to us.

85. ## Who writes history?

The history we've been taught dramatically distorts reality. James C. Scott reminds us that the history of the peasantry is written by city dwellers, that of nomadic people by those who have ceased to be so, that of hunter-gatherer peoples is written by those who engage in agriculture, and finally, the history of stateless peoples is written by court scribes. For established powers, all others are barbarians.

Given this, what Eduardo Galeano recounts in one of his books is hardly surprising.  He shows, for instance, that at the end of the 18th century, Napoleon's soldiers were surprised to discover that many Egyptian children believed the pyramids had been built by the French or the British. Two centuries later, many Japanese children believed that the atomic bombs dropped on Hiroshima and Nagasaki had been launched by the Russians. As a result of an infinity of movies shown over and over again – I add – in France there are some who think that it was the Americans alone who liberated Europe in 1944-1945. The Soviets, apparently, didn’t exist.

In light of all this, I'm reminded of the verses by Bertolt Brecht: “Who built Thebes of the seven gates ?  In the books you will read the names of kings. Did the kings haul up the lumps of rock ? And Babylon, many times demolished, who raised it up so many times ? In what houses of gold glittering Lima did its builders live ? Where, the evening that the Great Wall of China was finished, did the masons go? Great Rome is full of triumphal arches. Who erected them ? Over whom did the Caesars triumph ? Had Byzantium, much praised in song, only palaces for its inhabitants ? Even in fabled Atlantis, the night that the ocean engulfed it, the drowning still cried out for their slaves. The young Alexander conquered India. Was he alone ? Caesar defeated the Gauls. Did he not even have a cook with him ? Philip of Spain wept when his armada went down. Was he the only one to weep ?  Frederick the Great won the Seven Years’ War. Who else won it ? Every page a victory. Who cooked the feast for the victors ? Every ten years a great man. Who paid the bill ? So many reports.  So many questions.” And Brecht didn't seem to consider women much either.

86. ## Pitiful primitive societies

Years ago, after finishing a talk on degrowth in Logroño, a young man approached me and told me something he’d heard from a family member. If I remember correctly, that person was a Salesian nun. The nun had described, in angry tones, what she understood to be the condition of a primitive community that she’d had the opportunity (or misfortune) to know in some remote place in Venezuela.

As the story, though brief, perfectly illustrated how meanings and demands differ between distinct worlds, I took note of the features that the nun attributed to the primitive community. Here they are summarized: "No one can make them work; when they get the food they need, they spend a whole week talking. They live in a large hut. They have everything in common, and everyone takes care of everything; even the children. They go completely naked, so it's urgent to clothe them. In terms of sex, they do what they please and, as if that were not enough, they have some herbs that prevent women from ever getting pregnant."

I could never have imagined a world as extremely tedious as the one described. And never before did I understand as clearly why we persist in destroying such primitive, unhappy, and unproductive people. How much we have to teach them\!

87. ## The economy of the libido

I bring up, with an unmistakably humorous tone, a couple of anecdotes related to an economy, that of the libido, which also merits its own anti-capitalist or pre-capitalist version

Whenever I talk about degrowth, I try to emphasize that the logic of family is independent of competition and commodification. It's more commonly associated with donation, gifting, and gratitude. For instance, when parents pay for their children's education, they generally don't do it expecting some future economic return for themselves. But I also like to point out that the perspective of degrowth is neither sad nor gloomy. Most theories of degrowth, paradoxically perhaps, advocate for a general increase in sexual relations, justified by the need to recover the social life we have been squandering in our obsession with production and consumption.

A while back, after finishing a talk on degrowth, a man of around forty approached me and said he particularly liked one of my observations. When I asked which one, he said he really liked the idea that we should *fuck* more, and better – something I hadn't said literally – adding that he had a very serious piece of advice for me. "Say the bit about *fucking* at the beginning of your talk, and then go into all that stuff about self-management and direct democracy…”

Some time later, in a rather solemn setting, I again spoke about degrowth. No one warned me that sitting next to me was a jurist who was also a member of Opus Dei, a former deputy of the Popular Party in Spain, and an opponent of the abortion law. He was the first to speak in the debate. He pointed out \- albeit cordially \- that I had quite a chaotic mess in my head: on one hand, in his view, I defended the institution of the family; and on the other, I was advocating, also in his view, for outright libertinism. He also noted that my position was well-reflected in a sentence attributed to Woody Allen which he couldn't quite remember at the moment. Although I knew it – "Masturbation is fine, but sex is a great way to meet people" – the solemnity of the occasion led me to remain silent. I promise not to do that again.

88. ## Happiness in the Paleolithic

I have already pointed out that when the English Luddites began destroying machines during the first Industrial Revolution at the beginning of the 19th century, they faced unanimous condemnation. This criticism emphasized that the machines would be beneficial for everyone as they would shorten the workday and multiply wealth. However, nothing has confirmed such a claim: today, globally, those who have jobs are often forced to work more and more, often under worse conditions.

Many societies of the past don’t conform to the image of humans fighting desperately and working to exhaustion to survive. There was often a certain natural abundance, time dedicated to work was less, and the pressures exerted by life were reasonably bearable. The writings of an American anthropologist, Marshall Sahlins, suggest that in the Paleolithic era, daily chores required about the same amount of time as today's commute to work. For a more recent example, according to one estimate members of the peasantry in medieval Europe worked an average of 1,620 hours annually, while today a wage earner in the United States has to devote around 2,000 hours a year to work.

Work appears to have become more intense and demanding over the centuries, and mechanisms of control and pressure have intensified. While more and more people are unemployed, the general trend in affluent societies is, again, to *increase* the number of working hours. This is yet another of the paradoxes, to put it mildly, of contemporary capitalism.

89. ## France in the 1960s

When I started working on degrowth, I was deeply impressed by the views expressed at the time by a French essayist named Philippe Saint-Marc. Saint-Marc invited us to imagine a France where there were fewer than 200,000 unemployed, where crime rates were five times lower than today's, where hospitalizations related to mental disorders were reduced to a third of the current figure, where suicides decreased by 50 percent, and where drugs weren’t consumed. This idyllic world, apparently unattainable, was, however, according to Saint-Marc, the France of the 1960s... Similarly, it’s known that when a filmmaker wants to recreate a reasonably quiet urban world, he knows well that one of the easy options is to remove cars, a genuine contemporary plague, from streets and squares.

I dare to suggest that often, and paradoxically, the virtues we attribute to the past are simply the product of an austerity that was perhaps inevitable at the time. Regardless, it seems logical to conclude that more and more people are nostalgic for the economic and social life they once knew – I'm not, of course, referring to the *recent* past: it's also logical that, in times of crisis like the one we're experiencing, there are those who simplistically miss the relative, and false, prosperity of just a few years ago.

90. ## The value of time

This next anecdote takes place in a remote region of the Brazilian Amazon, inhabited by an indigenous community whose members primarily cut wood with extremely primitive tools. Missionaries who reached this region, noticing the hard work the indigenous people had to undertake, gave them stainless steel knives made in the United States. A couple of years later, the missionaries returned to the region, encountered the inhabitants again, and one of the first things they asked about was \- inevitably \- the knives. The response was immediate: it now took ten times less time to cut wood than before. Assuming then that the inhabitants were now producing ten times more wood, the missionary received an emphatic reply: they were obtaining the same amount of wood as before, and now enjoyed ten times more free time to devote to what objectively leads to well-being and happiness …

It seems as evident as it’s unfortunate that in our societies in the Northern hemisphere, we’ve lost the mental chip that many indigenous communities have preserved intact. A chip that – I must emphasize \- is perfectly rational and respectable. How often do we ask ourselves if the income we earn from an overload of work compensates for the rather meager pleasure provided by the goods and services we acquire?. Isn't time among the most precious commodities?

91. ## Ecology and rulers

The ecological awareness of our global leaders is remarkably weak. Usually, if not universally, they are wholly beholden to the interests of powerful economic and financial corporations operating behind the scenes. At best, they adopt the stance of green capitalism, which views ecology merely as an additional source of profit. Making matters worse, conventional politics is shockingly short-sighted, paying little or no attention to medium and long-term issues.

Let me recount an anecdote that, I believe, symbolically illustrates this. Its protagonist is Alfredo Pérez Rubalcaba, a socialist politician and at the time Vice President of the Spanish Government. In January 2011, he held a press conference in Madrid to promote a measure that, if we want to be generous, was a step in the right direction: the decision to reduce the maximum speed limit on highways and expressways from 120 to 110 kilometers per hour.

Four months later, the same individual called another press conference, to make two announcements.  First, the January measure had produced the desired effects: the Spanish energy bill had decreased significantly, and the number of fatalities on the roads had also notably declined. Pérez Rubalcaba’s second announcement was that, as a result, the decision had been made to restore the highway speed limit to 120 kilometers per hour.

One might have expected someone to pounce on Pérez Rubalcaba's neck for blatantly violating the most basic rules of formal logic. If a certain measure is implemented and announced to have yielded desired results, it seems logical to at the very least maintain that measure, or perhaps even extend it further. The \[right-wing\] Popular Party, which soon took over the Spanish government, was indeed flirting with the idea of raising the maximum speed limit to 130 kilometers per hour... A thing to behold.

92. ## An oxymoron: sustainable growth

Few words today are as pervasive and yet as vague as *sustainability.* I remember a few years ago, hearing a newscaster pronounce it on a television news program, my mother, already very elderly, commented with excellent clarity that this sustainability must mean nothing, as everyone is for it. There’s no self-respecting political party program that doesn’t proudly announce its commitment to sustainability. Even if the program in question fuels the crudest ecological aggressions.

I’ve no doubt that those who originally promoted the concept of sustainability did so with lucidity and good faith.  They were essentially saying that each new generation must hand over to the next—at least—the same natural capital that it inherited from the previous one.  I won't delve here into the controversies surrounding the concept of natural capital because I believe they are not immediately relevant right now.

A good indicator that a concept once considered disruptive has noticeably degraded over time is the fact that the former President \[i.e. Prime Minister\] of the Spanish Government, José Luis Rodríguez Zapatero, repeatedly fused the noun "growth" with the adjective "sustainable." In his view, it seems, the former gained substance as growth that is sustained or maintained over time. Regardless of Rodríguez Zapatero's views, at the stage we are in, especially in the wealthy North, the term "sustainable growth" inevitably seems like a contradiction in terms.

93. ## Care strike

Some time ago, during a rather mild general strike in Spain, a colleague and I considered the question of which areas of the economy the strike ought to affect. My friend, somewhat stuck in a past where production was, or seemed to be, everything, couldn't understand why we were also demanding a consumption strike. "What sense would it have," I asked him, "to stop work in factories and schools only to rush to shop at the supermarket later? And what about the rights of those working there?"

Often, however, just when we believe we’ve made a giant leap in understanding the world's problems, we soon realize we've fallen short. Because if the strike is to encompass production and consumption—and indeed, distribution, as the French yellow vests have taught us—what sense is there in talking about redistributing work if at the same time we ignore the vast scope of domestic labor, overwhelmingly carried out by women? Shouldn't we advocate, without reservations, for a strike that affects this type of work as well?

Antonella Picchio explains it clearly: "Feminist thought has shown us how the dominant economy renders much of the work done by women invisible. But the productive process is not sustained solely on the remunerated labor that produces goods and services; it also encompasses the unpaid work that facilitates social reproduction and the sustenance of the working class. Without domestic labor \- cooking to feed families, maintaining clothing and homes... \- and the care of people and their relationships, the economic system could not perpetuate itself. Yet, the growth economy does not consider this at all."

It’s hardly surprising that the idolized indicator of Gross Domestic Product (GDP) doesn’t care at all about domestic work. That indicator, and economic growth itself, have a patriarchal and male chauvinist character. In the eyes of Anselm Jappe, both are linked to masculine values \- toughness, determination, reason, calculation, contract \- while non-market activities are associated with feminine values: gentleness, understanding, emotion, and gratuity.

94. ## The bicycles wins

Our acceptance of increasingly complex societies has a very delicate correlate: we are becoming more and more dependent.  In response to this, I believe there’s no other remedy than to fight for a progressive simplification of the world we live in. Even so, I willingly admit that the concept of simplification has its problems, given that there are forms of complexity that deserve our full respect.

I'll try to explain what I mean, using a specific example: the contrast between the automobile and the bicycle. Manufacturing the former cannot be done by just anyone; it demands high technology accessible only to large corporations. When a car breaks down, we typically cannot repair it ourselves; we need to rely on specialized professionals. To get the car moving requires energy sources that often come from distant countries and are the root of many conflicts. And finally, there's the environmental aggression caused by cars and their associated industries.

In contrast, the circumstances surrounding the bicycle are, fortunately, different. Their production demands far less specialized and much more widespread technologies. It's a bit of an exaggeration, but it can be said that almost anyone is capable of making a bicycle. Repairs are often within our own capacity. And the only energy they require is that provided by our legs, which don’t pollute. There are ample reasons to conclude that, given these conditions, the bicycle is a much more suitable mode of transport than the car to face the challenges of imminent collapse.

95. ## Questions they don’t let us ask

One of the most significant abilities of the system we endure is how it prevents us from asking the truly important questions. I'll provide a very illustrative example of what I mean. 

The prevailing discourse insists that we must seek new energy raw materials to maintain and, if possible, enhance what we’ve achieved. So, what's the main question they manage to prevent us from asking? It goes more or less like this: Are we genuinely interested in preserving what we’ve achieved, or would we greatly benefit from doing away with many of its elements?

Let's suppose one day the President of the Russian Federation, Vladimir Putin, announces that his country has discovered a gigantic hydrocarbon reserve in the Arctic Ocean, capable of maintaining the current global energy consumption levels for an additional decade. Would anyone seriously think we'd use those ten years to critically rethink the irrationality plaguing our societies? I’ve no doubt that in a decade, we would have to face problems very similar to those facing us today. Or, in other words, we would need to revisit the questions that they stubbornly don’t let us ask.

96. ## A singularly pertinent referendum

I include here a text by Gustavo Duch, which, in line with what I just pointed out, reminds again that there are matters about which we’re not allowed to express ourselves. Although it plays stylistically on the aftermath of the Catalan referendum of October 2017, Duch in no way intends to diminish the significance of the latter. His aim is to draw attention to the importance of allowing us to express opinions on otherwise censored subjects, and not to belittle the significance of the Catalan referendum, then and today.

"After many demonstrations and no serious response, society decided to organize a referendum. Logistics such as where voting could take place, which census to use, where to acquire and hide ballot boxes, and how to transparently count the votes were easily resolved through self-management and social collaboration. The question to be posed was collectively decided in assembly processes. Everything was planned, except that on the day of the referendum, following orders from central governments, military and police forces arrived with batons and rubber bullets to prevent the illegal consultation."

"But the vote took place, and the results were known. The vast majority of the population from hundreds of countries responded 'yes' to the question on the ballots: 'In order to eradicate poverty, halt climate change, and avoid the sixth extinction, do you believe that it is necessary to end capitalism?'"

97. ## Ancient sociability

I have always thought that in Portugal an ancient style of sociability has been preserved that in many other places has evaporated. This phenomenon seems to still exist, despite showing signs of gradually receding, under the commercial pressures of the European Union and its associated miseries. I suspect this is happening at the same pace as the decline of the rural world, which is largely at the origin of this type of sociability.

The example of this thesis that comes to mind, however, doesn’t refer to the rural world. Instead, it’s something that took place in a modern train, a product of perhaps the latest technology, connecting the two major Portuguese cities: Porto and Lisbon. In other words, I’m not talking about a 19th-century wooden train.

I was reading in my seat, and after leaving Coimbra behind, I overheard a conversation in the corridor between the conductor and an elderly lady who was speaking in French. The lady, intending to travel north to Porto, had mistakenly boarded a train heading to Lisbon. Half horrified, she asked the conductor what the next stop was. He replied that Lisbon was next, meaning there were no intermediate stations.

In Spain, I’m sure, the conversation would’ve ended there. If someone, very boldly, had dared to ask about the possibility of the train making an extraordinary stop at an intermediate station, they would probably have received from the conductor, in an unfriendly tone, a response like: "Don't ask foolish questions. The computer system doesn't allow it, madam\!" In Portugal, however, the issue was quickly resolved. The conductor called the train driver, explained the situation, and asked to stop at the next station. The driver did so.  We arrived in Lisbon with an unforgivable delay of three minutes. Damn that ancient sociability.

98. ## Nuclear salvation

I don't know the author of the phrase, but it’s widely acclaimed, and goes roughly like this: "I am a firm supporter of the complete abolition of the death penalty, except in the case of architects." I allow myself to replace “architects” with radio and television commentators. You know the ones: those individuals, the *todólogos* \[know-it-alls; literally, everything-ologists\], who, in their most generous moments, begin their remarks by confessing they know nothing about the topic they are about to cover, only to immediately demonstrate that, for once, they were entirely right and indeed knew nothing.

A few years ago, while watching a discussion on a television channel, I was amazed to see one of the usual *todólogos* – a person accustomed to abusing the Spanish language, fond of nebulous arguments, and always servile to the powerful – appear to be quite inspired. I'm not being ironic: he produced what I considered an extremely lucid, serious, and meticulous reflection on the delicate panorama we face due to the exhaustion of all the energy raw materials we use today. When he finished his intervention, I understood what was happening: this sharp reflection was aimed expressly at showing that there was only one solution. It's easy to guess what that was: nuclear energy. I don't think I'm wrong in suggesting that this gentleman had dined the night before with a press officer of a nuclear industry company, who had instructed him on what to argue to justify the conclusion that this form of energy was the only viable way out.

Let’s not let ourselves be deceived.  According to the commonly cited data, there is enough uranium on the planet for 35 to 40 years. Those who want to make nuclear energy our lifeline claim that we need to at least triple the current number of nuclear power plants. The arithmetic is simple: we'll have uranium for 12 to 15 years. No one knows what to do with the waste generated by these plants, which is yet another awful legacy for future generations. The construction of these plants is very costly in terms of climate change, the energy they produce is universally expensive and almost always requires costly public subsidies. Not to mention the safety issues of the plants, a debate intensified by the Japanese accident some years ago. Our *todólogo* friend should eat less, read more, and, above all, talk to different people.

99. ## He left power wearing the same coat

Egalitarian behaviors and codes don’t die. So much so that some people feel a genuine fascination in confirming their persistence after centuries of capitalism, statehood, and patriarchy. To illustrate this persistence, this time I won’t travel to the Southern hemisphere; instead, I'll head to the very singular space that is Central and Eastern Europe.

In the late 1990s, during a trip to Moscow, a Russian friend living in Madrid asked me to bring her mother a package of clothes and food. I arrived at the home of this kind lady, located in a modest neighborhood on the outskirts of the city. It quickly became apparent that my friend's mother had significant reasons to reject the former Soviet order.  Her relatives had suffered the effects of the brutal repression during the Stalin years.

At one point, I asked this lady what she thought of the leaders who were in power at that time, or just before, in Central and Eastern Europe. She began a long response in which she demonized each of these leaders in turn. She referred in an unfriendly tone to Gorbachev, who, in her view, had arrived at the Kremlin as a modest man, only to soon be dressed by flashy tailors and enjoy two formidable mansions. She said something similar about Yeltsin, with the inevitable addition of a dark comment about the first president of independent Russia's fondness for alcohol. After bitterly criticizing other political figures, she paused and said, "Of all these people, there's only one I trust." "And who is that?" I asked. "Tadeusz Mazowiecki, the former Polish Prime Minister. He came to power with a coat and left power wearing that same coat," she replied.

It's striking that someone who would’ve been placed far from an egalitarian worldview, when it came time to judge the leaders of her country and others nearby, had no hesitation in proudly employing that very worldview. Some might be tempted to say that this egalitarian undercurrent, this rejection of profiteering and private enrichment, was merely a legacy of the Soviet era. I doubt it. Soviet-type systems were anything but egalitarian, and the egalitarian code clearly existed in the collective imagination even before their emergence. One only needs to look at what the 19th-century Russian populists, the *Narodniks*, advocated, and what is postulated, amidst a sea of contradictions, by the autocephalous Orthodox Church itself.

100. ## The “we” of Marcos

I don't know which word is most used in Spanish. When speaking, and especially at the beginning of sentences, I suspect it is *yo* \[I\]. Although some might argue, quite reasonably, that this is logical, I prefer to view mountains and seas from a different perspective and suggest that "yo" unfortunately displaces *nosotros* or *nosotras* \[we, masculine and feminine\], and that it often reflects the traits of societies burdened by a regrettable individualism. This text by Subcomandante Marcos gives us food for thought on this matter.

"According to our cultural tradition, the world was created by several gods. Gods who were very much into dancing, very explosive \- as we also say \- who didn't do a complete job. They left things hanging, or things that were done poorly. One of these was that they did not make all men and women upright, that is, with a good heart. Instead, some governor or some president of the country ended up with an evil soul and a crooked heart. When the gods realized this injustice, that there were men and women living at the expense of others, they wanted to help the “corn people” – the indigenous peoples of this country. And to help them, they removed a word: they removed the 'I.' In indigenous communities, in those of Mayan roots and in many peoples of this country, the word 'I' does not exist. In its place, 'we' is used. In our Mayan languages, it's 'tic.' This 'tic' ending, which refers to the collective or community, is repeated over and over again. And nowhere does 'I' appear. 'We are not afraid to die fighting,' we say. We never speak in singular. The 'tic,' which repeats itself over and over in our languages, is like the tick-tock of the clock we want to carry, to be part of this country, without being a shame for it, an affront or a reason for mockery or charity."

101. ## Sweeping one’s own house

"I have noticed," said Mr. K, "that many people are put off by our teaching, because we know the answer to everything. Couldn't we, in the interests of propaganda, draw up a list of questions which appear to us quite unresolved?" Bertolt Brecht wrote this insightfully in his *Geschichten vom Herrn Keuner* (Stories of Mr. Keuner).

Someone clever might respond that such a list would be so long it would irreparably damage the propaganda. On the contrary, acknowledging our limitations seems like a good principle, both in formulating and answering questions. A good principle that also advises to wholeheartedly accept it's unwise to boast about anything. We must first start by discussing what a doctrine is and whether it is healthy to have one, or, at least, to be absorbed by it. Especially if the doctrine in question, as often happens, seems imposed or imported.

Some might suggest that in many areas, paradoxically, our best attributes are our flaws and shortcomings. And perhaps that's why it's unhealthy to despise anyone, and it’s advisable to first sweep one's own house. For hygiene.

# Epilogue.  Pre-capitalists, anti-capitalists

The reader will allow me, very briefly, to shed some light on what I understand to be behind many of the stories in this work. I’ll do this by means of two categories that I must acknowledge from the outset are sufficiently nebulous to encompass whatever we might subjectively deem appropriate, and exclude what, just as subjectively, doesn’t interest us.

The first of these categories, which will receive the bulk of my attention here, is associated with the world, societies, and cultures of the *pre-capitalist* era. It’s hardly an unproblematic category. For one, it seems to place capitalism at the center of history, envisaging societies as either pre-capitalist or, perhaps, post-capitalist. It also seems to assume, against all evidence, that a variety of extremely different realities can be encompassed under a single term. Finally, we must not be naive about the past, and remember that while there are aspects of pre-capitalist societies that are positive, these have often been accompanied by other qualities that are less so.

As for the second category, that of *anti-capitalism*, it also comes with its own set of complications, largely tied to the question of the degree to which the object of investigation is truly anti-capitalist. For example, while the social democracy of a century ago had a vision that could legitimately be called anti-capitalist, it seems impossible to say the same of the contemporary social-democracy. Much of the institutional left – I'm aware that the term is problematic – appears to be more anti-neoliberal than genuinely anti-capitalist. What, then, to make of the Soviet-type systems, often described as forms of state bureaucratic capitalism?

Despite these and perhaps other shortcomings, I believe it's useful to retain these two categories for purposes of classification. I often venture to suggest – as I do in one of the texts included in this work – that a significant part of the enduring appeal of the anarchist movement in Spain before 1939 is its manifestly spontaneous blend of pre-capitalist and anti-capitalist elements. The former is likely a consequence of the fact that its members came from rural areas, or were descendants of people from such backgrounds, who preserved in their mindset and behavior many aspects of collective organization and mutual aid.  This was coupled to the powerful influence of an ideology, anarchism, that emerged in 19th-century Europe and significantly strengthened pre-capitalist elements through a reciprocal influence.  A similar hybridization phenomenon is visible in recent initiatives like those in Chiapas and Rojava, among many examples.

Whatever the case, I want to emphasize that the pre-capitalist impulse has a more experiential and material dimension than the anti-capitalist one, where the weight of ideology is undoubtedly heavier. As Raúl Iturra says wisely, "the oral tradition of the peasant is not found in what he says, but in what he does.”  I also believe that by establishing a future combination of these two impulses, we will gain a compelling guide for navigating the stormy seas of revolutions and emancipations.  My point here is that in many of the stories collected in this book it’s relatively easy to see the imprint of a pre-capitalist impulse – its strength is greater in the peasant world and in Southern countries – whereas at other times, we detect the influence of an anti-capitalist viewpoint.

I’m compelled to delve into the nature of the pre-capitalist universe, which ultimately is what interests me here. In his writings, E. P. Thompson often used the concept of *moral economy* to refer to a set of realities that, in conditions characterized by the absence of nationalisms and of homogenizing religious orthodoxies, gave rise to a vast diversity of practices. These emerged under norms that were not necessarily in line with those dictated by the political or religious authorities of the time. In essence, the moral economy aimed, and aims, to protect the community against risk and often became manifest as resistance to technical innovations – recall the significant role of Luddism – and against the rationalizations of the market. Paul Greenough has defined moral economy as "the cluster of relations of exchange between social groups, and between persons, in which the welfare and the merit of both parties to the exchange take precedence over considerations such as the profit of the one or the other." Many of Thompson's works identify in 18th-, and particularly in 19th-century England, a confrontation between the emerging market economy on one side,  and the moral economy of the common people on the other, which resisted the intrusions of the bourgeoisie and the clergy.

The resistances in question were primarily materialized as collective work, thus blurring the separation between work and life, which in practice were intermingled. Moreover, in a context where self-subsistence was the general norm, the purpose of working wasn’t to benefit the market. This clearly highlighted the logic of solidarity, and of various forms democratic and assembly-oriented organizations, similar to the councils that still exist among us. Additionally, networks extended beyond the family, supporting a collective, cooperative effort.  Importantly, in social organization, and in subsequent uprisings, women had an active presence that later vanished into the market economy, with its fictitious universalist and liberating claims. The forms of resistance I discuss often appeared as irony, mockery, and subtle acts of defiance against the powerful, as might be illustrated in some of the stories in this book.

It's true that not everything glittered in the glow of this moral economy. In Western Europe, at least, the moral economy developed in an environment to varying degrees influenced by the interests of nobles, lords, landowners, and clergy, who sought to maintain many characteristics of societies in which inequality was clearly pervasive. Although there was an intent to provide some degree of security and to ensure a minimum income and land access, which did foster egalitarian principles to some extent, and though the wealthy were willing to sacrifice a portion of their profits to avoid unrest and rebellion, it is clear that repressive mechanisms serving the powerful persisted.

In tune with the interpretation I've suggested regarding pre-1939 Spanish anarchism, Michael Löwy has made an effort to highlight significant links between the world of pre-capitalist moral economy and the culture of the working class that emerged in the 19th century. Löwy cites Richard Hoggart in this regard, who has pointed out that many of the roots of working-class culture are indeed peasant in nature. Working-class culture has largely drawn from the values of local and family community, and affective ties, as opposed to modernity, which always refers to a society burdened by commerce, competition, selfishness, and corruption. This doesn't negate Peter Laslett's assertion that the advent of the modern world ended another world "in which entire life unfolded within the family, amid a circle of familiar and beloved faces, of objects known and cherished, all within man's reach." I'll add that while Marx and Engels saw no merit in imagining a persistent tradition of past phenomena informing present struggles, Raymond Williams argued that pre-capitalist values should play a central role in emancipatory processes Perhaps; and regarding Marx, a note of caution: in his last years, as luminous as they are overlooked, he fortunately shifted his stance on issues like these, to some extent, in line with Williams' suggestions.

Indeed, Williams' assertion becomes more comprehensible when applied to the Global South, where, in many aspects, the moral economy has shown great strength in the past—evident in the numerous indigenous communities that have practiced libertarian or liberating ways—and continues to exist in the present. Serge Latouche suggests that the informal economy prevalent in many Southern countries reflects the dichotomy between *a-economy,* referring to intelligent management at the domestic level, and *chrematistics*, which seeks unlimited accumulation. Latouche notes that Ivan Illich used the term *vernacular a-economy* to denote popular practices of survival, which, properly speaking, don't constitute a third sector but rather an extra-sectorial universe. Latouche himself speaks of the existence in the Global South, alongside *capitalist and public sectors*, of a *relational* pole, organized around human values and based on social bonds and communal ownership.

It’s crucial, however, to recognize that categories like these have different meanings or are based on different foundations in Africa, Asia, and America. Primary sociability seems more significant in Africa, where clan systems and family solidarity persist, and where Western modernity has less penetration. It is true, however, that in referring to three *continents* I have used three terms which conceal the fact that behind each of them lie very diverse realities. Certainly, the reality of indigenous communities in the Bolivian highlands is not the same as that prevalent in Lima or Iquique, just as the characteristics of the hill tribes in Southeast Asia differ from those in Saigon or Bangkok.

I'd like to draw three succinct conclusions. First, we should dismiss the notion, dear to Hobsbawm and many Marxist historians, that peasants and artisans are inevitably victims swimming against the current of history. This current has produced such despicable offshoots that one must navigate cautiously when establishing norms in such murky terrain. While this first conclusion does recognize the victimhood of peasants and perhaps artisans, it questions the wisdom of simply accepting that history must, unequivocally, sweep them away.

Secondly, while the values and practices typical of the pre-capitalist moral economy today have a very reduced presence in the rich and urban North of the planet, the same cannot be said for the poorer, often rural South. And thirdly, in line with a thesis adopted by the oft-mentioned Thompson, I suggest that even if envisioning a return to the pre-capitalist world may not make sense —although I'm not sure if an imaginable general collapse might not make us question this— that scenario can still provide alternative horizons for our slim possibilities of action. That is what some of the stories I have tried to bring to the minds of those who have read these pages aspire to.
